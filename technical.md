# Technical Debrief

*The map after the walk.*

The narrative book ends with **“The Drawer Comes Out.”** What follows is not a continuation of the story and not a summary of it. The thirty-seven chapters have already done the primary work: they have placed the reader inside episodes of repair, recognition, disagreement, memory, skill, interpretation, obligation, modelling, translation, collective work and continuity through change.

This section makes explicit the structure those episodes have earned.

The distinctions below do not all have the same status. Some are strongly supported by the narrative. Some are useful working analyses. Some are proposals that remain open to revision. A few are deliberately left unresolved because the difficult cases are part of the evidence, not noise to be removed.

Four labels will be useful:

- **Established here** means the narrative gives repeated, varied support for the claim.
- **Plausible** means the narrative supports the claim, but not strongly enough to treat it as settled.
- **Proposed** means the technical account introduces a useful formulation that goes beyond what the stories alone establish.
- **Open** means the book has exposed a real question without resolving it.

The purpose of these labels is not caution for its own sake. It is to keep the map answerable to the landscape.

## Technical Chapter One: What Changed?

The book began with a drawer that would not close. That made it easy, at first, to think the subject was problem-solving.

It is not.

A problem can certainly become intelligible. A fault can be found. A question can receive an answer. But later chapters made the same underlying phenomenon visible in cases where nothing was broken and no explicit question had been asked. Mara could already play the notes before the musical phrase became audible to her as a phrase. Hannah could hear every word of a joke without yet getting it. Ruth could understand something new about her mother without resolving grief. Ravi and Mae could learn to see more in a jug without reaching one final aesthetic verdict.

The most durable question turned out to be simpler:

**What changed?**

### 1. Understanding as a changed relation to a situation

The strongest generalization the narrative supports is this:

**When something becomes more intelligible, the person's relation to the situation changes.**

That change may show itself in what the person can now:

- notice;
- distinguish;
- connect;
- expect;
- test;
- question;
- say;
- perform;
- rely on;
- refuse;
- revise;
- teach;
- continue.

This is broader than solving a problem and narrower than merely undergoing a psychological change.

A useful operational term is **availability**. Something is available to a person when it can enter their present thinking or action in a way that matters to what they are doing. A distinction can become available. So can a relation, a possible move, a warning, a counterexample, a remembered question, a way of testing, a reason not to decide yet, or a way of hearing a phrase.

This is not the claim that understanding is a substance called “availability.” It is a way of describing a recurring before-and-after difference.

Before Ben and Nora notice the drawer dropping, they remove spoons. After the dropping becomes available to them, they inspect the runner.

Before Mara hears the cello line, her own notes are a sequence she can execute. Afterward, those same notes are heard as continuing something already underway.

Before Celia's reading group becomes precise about the story, “hope” and “grief” are competing labels. Afterward, the group can say which details support which reading, what an author interview settles, and what it does not.

Before Mina understands the range of the pendulum approximation, a failed prediction looks like a failed formula. Afterward, the question becomes whether the model has been used outside the conditions under which it is a good approximation.

In each case, what becomes available changes what can reasonably happen next.

That claim is **established here**.

But one qualification is essential.

A changed capacity for response is not, by itself, enough to define understanding. Conditioning can change behaviour. Habit can change response. A person can copy a gesture, follow a cue, or produce the expected answer without understanding what the response is answerable to. The stagehands in “Same Direction” coordinate successfully for a while while misunderstanding the basis of their coordination. Amira can initially give a correct multiplication answer before she understands why the pattern continues.

So a changed relation is best treated as a **signature** of understanding, not a complete definition.

A stronger proposed formulation is:

**Understanding involves a change in what is available to a person or group, where that changed availability is organized in a way that remains answerable to the matter being understood.**

“Answerable” will be made more precise in Technical Chapter Three.

### 2. What understanding is not identical with

Several nearby achievements often accompany understanding. None is identical with it.

| Nearby notion | Why it is not the same thing |
| --- | --- |
| Possessing information | Amira can possess “7 × 8 = 56” before understanding why it follows. |
| Having a true belief | A person can believe something true for a poor reason, while a false explanation can still organize a situation intelligibly enough to guide action for a time. |
| Remembering | Nora can use what an earlier repair changed without remembering the original episode in detail. |
| Agreeing | A whole office can agree about the fuse and be wrong; two people can disagree while understanding exactly what the disagreement concerns. |
| Explaining | Aisha can ride well while explaining balance badly; Hannah can get a joke before she can explain why it landed. |
| Performing | Correct performance can precede fuller understanding, as with Mara's notes or rote multiplication. |
| Recognizing | Recognition is one important form of change, but not every case is recognition. |
| Predicting | Prediction can be part of understanding, but a map, promise, skill or aesthetic judgment need not primarily predict. |
| Coordinating | Eli and Sana can coordinate while holding incompatible accounts of their cue. |
| Solving | Music, grief, interpretation and aesthetic attention can deepen without a solved problem. |
| Deciding | Sometimes progress consists in refusing to decide prematurely. |
| Having a representation | A representation may support understanding, but possessing the map, formula, recipe or record does not guarantee understanding it. |

These distinctions matter because the book is not trying to identify one hidden mental item present in every case. It is identifying a family of changes through which a situation becomes more intelligible in a way that can matter afterward.

### 3. Six candidate forms of change

The narrative proposed six recurring descriptions of what changes when something becomes intelligible:

1. **A difference becomes salient or usable.**
2. **Something becomes possible or available.**
3. **A relation becomes intelligible.**
4. **A mismatch, fault or failure is put right.**
5. **Several elements become organized into a functioning whole.**
6. **A live question receives an answer or sufficient determination.**

These are useful analytical distinctions. They are not established as six mutually exclusive kinds of event.

#### A difference becomes salient

The two train lines leaving at the same time become distinguishable by destination. The two almost-identical desk screws become distinguishable by length. The first drawer problem shifts when “something inside” and “one side dropping” cease to be treated as the same possibility.

The important word is not merely *difference*. Many differences are present all the time. The change occurs when a difference becomes available as relevant to what is happening.

#### Something becomes possible or available

A person can now make the bread, follow the route, ride the bicycle, hear the phrase, use a model within its range, or ask a better question.

This form is broad enough to risk swallowing the others. Almost every understanding changes what becomes possible in some sense. It is therefore likely better treated as a common consequence of understanding than as one coordinate category alongside all the others.

That is an **open structural issue**.

#### A relation becomes intelligible

The ceiling patch relates to a pipe rather than merely to the flat above. Mara's line relates to the cello and clarinet. The metro diagram preserves order and interchange while sacrificing geographic distance. A promise relates earlier words to later actions that can count as keeping or breaking it.

Many of the book's strongest later cases belong here.

#### A mismatch or failure is put right

The drawer closes. The heating circulates. A mistaken fuse diagnosis is abandoned. A translation is improved. A model is brought back within its range or replaced by a more suitable calculation.

Yet repair is rarely primitive. Repair often depends on first seeing a difference, relation or failed integration. In that sense, “repair” may describe the larger episode while another category describes the mechanism through which repair became possible.

#### Several elements become organized into a functioning whole

The desk is assembled. A recipe becomes usable through words, touch, timing and correction. An exhibition emerges from curatorial, conservation, spatial, linguistic and safety constraints that no one person contains.

Again, integration can overlap strongly with relation and repair.

#### A live question receives an answer or sufficient determination

Which platform? Whose parcel? Did the shop open on 13 July 1968? Does the counterexample defeat the claim?

This description works well where there is an explicit question. But “You Know This Part” shows why it cannot be the universal model. Mara's musical understanding deepens before there is a well-formed question to answer. “Nothing to Fix” does the same from another direction: Ruth becomes more able to understand aspects of her mother's habits without the episode culminating in an answer.

Settlement may therefore be a higher-level description that sometimes accompanies the other changes rather than a sixth mechanism beside them.

### 4. Overlap is not an embarrassment

The later narrative makes it difficult to maintain clean borders between these six descriptions.

That is useful evidence.

The thermostat episode involves differentiation, relation, failed repair and revised integration of facts.

The bread recipe involves relation, ability, coordination among cues, and correction.

The reading group does not simply “answer” the story. It differentiates interpretations, relates passages to claims, and improves the structure of uncertainty.

The staircase is physically repaired while its identity becomes less rather than more simple.

The exhibition is integrated through distributed work, but that integration depends on physical constraints, linguistic correction, institutional purposes and partial knowledge.

If a single episode can be described accurately in several ways, there are at least three possibilities:

1. the categories overlap but remain useful;
2. some categories operate at different levels of description;
3. the candidate list needs revision.

The technical account should not decide among these simply because a neat taxonomy would be convenient.

### 5. Recognition without a prior problem

One of the most important corrections produced by the later chapters is that understanding need not begin with consciously felt unfinishedness.

Mara can already play the notes.

Mateo can hear the words of the joke.

Ravi can already see the jug.

Ruth is not trying to solve a defined question when the bus ticket changes how she sees her mother's habit of keeping scraps.

In such cases, intelligibility enlarges.

That suggests a broader starting point than:

    something needs dealing with

A more durable formulation is:

    something becomes newly available as intelligible

That new availability may answer a question. It may reveal that there was a question. It may create a new question. Or it may simply enlarge what can now be noticed.

This is one reason “What changed?” has proved more robust than “What problem was solved?”

### 6. A precise but non-final formulation

The strongest formulation currently earned by the book is:

**An episode of making sense involves a change in a person's or group's relation to a situation such that relevant distinctions, relations, possibilities, expectations, tests, actions or continuations become available in a way they were not before, while remaining answerable to what the episode is about.**

Several terms in that sentence remain deliberately broad.

“Episode” does not imply that understanding always has a sharp beginning or end.

“Group” does not imply a group mind.

“Situation” may include physical objects, texts, mathematical relations, promises, institutional practices, fictional structures and other people.

“Available” does not mean verbally explicit.

“Answerable” does not mean that every domain answers back in the same way.

The sentence is therefore a working account, not a final definition.

It is nevertheless more precise than the book's original starting image of an unresolved problem reaching a useful ending.

The stories forced that change.

## Technical Chapter Two: Enough, But Enough For What?

“Enough” appears early in the book as a simple practical word.

Enough salt.

A secure enough curtain rail.

Enough evidence to stop blaming the thermostat.

Enough information to board the right train.

As the book widens, the word becomes more demanding.

A metro map can be enough for choosing an interchange and not enough for estimating walking distance. A pendulum approximation can be enough within a range and misleading outside it. A reading group can understand a story better without reaching one interpretation. A moral conflict can become clearer without becoming easier. Ruth can understand something more truthfully while nothing is repaired.

The technical lesson is not that standards are vague.

It is that **finite understanding is always sufficient or insufficient relative to some demand, use, question, risk or continuation**—and sometimes no single “enough” marks the end of the episode at all.

### 1. Sufficiency is not truth

Truth and sufficiency can interact, but they answer different questions.

A claim can be true and insufficient for the task.

“The thermostat is stopping the boiler” is true in one respect. It is insufficient as an explanation of the fault.

A model can be deliberately incomplete and still sufficient for a prediction within a defined range.

A map can be accurate about connectivity and misleading about physical distance.

A promise can be clearly understood while its fulfilment remains uncertain.

A story can be well understood while more than one interpretation remains live.

This gives the first distinction:

**Truth asks whether a claim is correct. Sufficiency asks whether what is presently available is enough for a specified continuation or demand.**

Neither reduces to the other.

### 2. Sufficiency is not certainty

Nora and Leila do not know the future life of the curtain rail. They test the fixing under loads relevant to its intended use and stop.

The train passengers do not acquire certainty about the whole rail system. They acquire enough information to know which platform to remain on and what will happen to their tickets after the missed connection.

The shop photograph yields an opening date without proving that the photograph was taken on the first day.

A responsible stopping point can therefore coexist with remaining uncertainty.

The relevant contrast is not:

    uncertain / certain

but often:

    not warranted to continue / warranted to continue

This is why understanding uncertainty can itself count as progress.

Celia's reading group becomes better able to say what is settled, what supports each interpretation, and what remains underdetermined. The result is not certainty. It is a more disciplined field of uncertainty.

### 3. Sufficiency is not completion

Completion suggests that there is nothing relevant left to do.

Many useful endings in the book are weaker.

The soup can be served.

The journey can continue.

The repair can be left alone.

The explanation can be used provisionally.

The model can be applied within its range.

The group can decide not to decide.

The person can stop because further action would be unsafe.

A useful term here is **release**: a prior concern no longer governs the next move.

When the guard says the tickets will be accepted on the later train, the delay remains. What changes is that the ticket question no longer controls the journey.

When Karim learns the stain is old, the stain remains. The assumption of an active leak stops governing what he does next.

When Ruth decides “not yet” about the coat, nothing is resolved in a final sense. But an immediate demand to decide is suspended.

Release is important.

It is also structurally awkward.

### 4. The candidate sufficiency categories are probably not coordinate

The early scheme proposed three broad ways a change might become enough:

1. one particular feature or consideration decides the matter;
2. several features jointly provide sufficient support;
3. something that had been controlling the next move stops controlling it.

The first two describe the **distribution of support**.

A single decisive observation can settle which runner is moving.

Several observations together can support a leak diagnosis.

The third describes something else: the **effect on the live situation**.

A single feature can produce release. Several features can produce release. Release can therefore cross the focal/distributed distinction rather than sit beside it.

This is one of the clearest places where the 6 × 3 × 3 structure fails to earn its apparent neatness.

A better provisional analysis separates two questions:

**How is the change supported?**

- primarily by one decisive consideration;
- by several considerations jointly;
- by some more complex or iterated structure not captured by that contrast.

**What does the resulting change do to the live situation?**

- settles or determines something;
- permits an action or continuation;
- removes a prior obstacle or concern from control;
- sharpens an uncertainty;
- opens a new question;
- changes the terms of the disagreement;
- perhaps something else.

This revised analysis is **proposed**, not established.

Its main virtue is that it stops treating support and practical effect as though they were the same dimension.

### 5. Enough for what?

The question “enough for what?” is not a loophole through which any standard can be made acceptable.

It is a demand for specificity.

The soup can be enough to serve without being ideal for every diner.

The curtain fixing can be enough for normal use without being enough for a child to climb.

The metro map can be enough for line order while being poor evidence of street distance.

The pendulum approximation can be enough inside a small-angle range while being unsuitable for a large swing.

The phrase “close enough” has content only when the tolerated difference and the purpose are specified.

Purpose does not make the world obedient.

Calling a broken brake “good enough for stopping” does not make it stop.

Calling a flooded road “passable enough for driving” does not move the water.

Calling an interpretation “valid for me” does not make it compatible with every sentence in the story.

Calling a calculation “adequate for our purposes” does not rescue it when the error is larger than the purpose permits.

Purpose-relativity therefore has to remain answerable to the thing being done.

### 6. Local adequacy and unrestricted validity

Several later chapters expose the difference between an account that is good within a range and an account that claims too much.

The pendulum model is the clearest case.

The simple formula is not treated as an embarrassed approximation waiting to be replaced by the “real” one. It is useful because, within a range, it preserves relations that matter and suppresses complications that do not yet matter.

But the range belongs to competent use of the model.

Forget the range and the same formula becomes misleading.

The metro map makes the same point with representation rather than prediction.

It preserves order and interchange at the cost of geographic likeness.

If a traveller asks it for walking distance, the problem is not simply that the map is false. The traveller has asked the representation to preserve a relation it was designed to sacrifice.

This suggests:

**Understanding a representation includes some understanding of what kinds of question it is fit to answer.**

That claim is **strongly supported** by the later narrative.

### 7. Better understanding can make action less obvious

The moral conflict in “We Agree on What Happened” matters because it blocks another tempting simplification.

Sometimes understanding does not make the next action easier.

Nessa and Daniel agree about the facts. They understand the competing responsibilities more clearly as the conversation develops. The difficulty remains because the difficulty is not caused by ignorance alone.

Similarly, aesthetic disagreement can become richer without converging.

The reading group can become more precise without selecting a winner.

Ruth can understand more while grief becomes more, not less, complicated.

This means sufficiency cannot be treated as the universal endpoint of understanding.

A better claim is:

**Enoughness is one important relation between understanding and continuation, not the definition of understanding's successful completion.**

Sometimes the relevant gain is instead:

- a sharper uncertainty;
- a better-framed disagreement;
- recognition of a limit;
- awareness of competing obligations;
- a new capacity to notice;
- a new question;
- a more truthful refusal to close the matter.

### 8. Understanding can preserve uncertainty

One of the book's central claims can now be stated directly:

**Understanding does not have to abolish uncertainty in order to reorganize what it is reasonable or possible to do next.**

That sentence applies to practical repair, historical evidence, fiction, scientific modelling, moral conflict and grief in different ways.

It does not imply that uncertainty is always valuable.

Some uncertainty can and should be removed.

The point is narrower:

**uncertainty and understanding are not opposites.**

A person can understand exactly what remains uncertain, why it remains uncertain, which possibilities are still live, and what would count for or against them.

That may be the most responsible form of understanding available.

## Technical Chapter Three: What Remains, and How Can It Still Be the Same Matter?

The first drawer repair disappears from attention almost as soon as dinner resumes.

That is normal.

Understanding does not usually remain as a vivid replay of the episode that produced it.

Yet later episodes can still be changed by what happened earlier.

This is where the book's deepest technical problem begins.

**What remains?**

And when what remains has changed form, wording, medium, person or implementation:

**in what sense is the later occasion still answerable to the same matter?**

### 1. There is no single thing called “what remains”

The book gives many different answers.

A **settled result** can remain: the shop opened on 13 July 1968.

A **relation** can remain: this line connects to that one; this promise bears on that later action.

A **skill** can remain: riding a bicycle, handling dough, playing a phrase differently.

A **habit** can remain: looking for a tripped breaker before guessing at random.

A **memory** can remain, accurately or inaccurately.

A **record** can remain: a note, recipe card, photograph, video, label or rule.

An **obligation** can remain: a promise can continue to matter after the words have stopped being present.

A **question** can remain: was the photograph taken on opening day?

A **warning** can remain until the danger changes.

A **model** can remain usable inside a range.

A **practice** can remain while its tools and procedures change.

A **changed disposition** can remain: a readiness to test a first explanation rather than defend it.

An **institutional rule** can remain partly through changed implementation.

A **distributed achievement** can remain in coordinated work no individual could reproduce alone.

These should not be collapsed into “stored knowledge.”

They have different media, different failure modes and different ways of being taken up later.

### 2. Remainders can change form

The bread recipe moves through handwriting, spoken instruction, touch, timing, a video and another person's attempt.

The promise moves through changed wording and later action.

Elena's translation preserves something by refusing literal wording.

The rowing rule survives partly because its implementation changes.

The staircase remains the staircase partly because physical material is replaced.

The pendulum model remains useful because its simplifications are understood rather than forgotten.

Lia's fictional character becomes constrained by previously established scenes even though the author is free to rewrite them.

Anna and Beth's memories are corrected by a video without collapsing into one complete record of the evening.

The exhibition exists as an intelligible achievement although no one participant knows the whole set of reasons that produced it.

The original drawer disappears into a skip while a way of testing remains active years later.

Literal preservation is therefore only one special case of continuity.

Often continuity requires transformation.

### 3. A working heuristic for same-matter continuity

The narrative repeatedly approaches the following test:

**A later event may still concern the same matter when what happens later can genuinely answer, contradict, correct, fulfil, continue, explain, test, realize or transform what was earlier at issue.**

This is useful.

It is not yet a definition.

Its strength is practical.

A payment can fulfil an earlier debt.

A later measurement can contradict an earlier model prediction.

A new photograph can answer part of an inherited historical question.

A person's later action can count as keeping or breaking a promise.

A revised translation can be criticized for losing something the source text established.

A changed rule can be challenged for no longer serving the purpose the earlier practice was organized around.

These relations show that later events are not merely similar to earlier ones. They can bear on them.

### 4. Answerability

The central technical term proposed by this debrief is **answerability**.

A later thought, action, record, representation or practice is answerable to an earlier matter when it is not free to declare itself a faithful continuation merely by assertion. Something independent of that assertion remains capable of counting for or against it.

This independence need not mean “physically external to every person.”

The source of resistance differs by domain.

| Domain | What can count for or against the understanding |
| --- | --- |
| Physical repair | behaviour of the object, load, motion, failure, measurement |
| Empirical inquiry | observation, measurement, evidence, prediction |
| Mathematics | definitions, proof, counterexample, calculation |
| Communication | another person's uptake, correction, intention, response |
| Promise or obligation | the undertaking, later action, the claims of affected people |
| Historical memory | records, testimony, surviving objects, conflicting evidence |
| Textual interpretation | words, structure, genre, established facts of the text |
| Aesthetic practice | perceivable relations, skilled comparison, use, context, developed criticism |
| Institutional practice | stated purpose, consequences, procedures, people subject to the rule |
| Fiction under construction | already established characterization, events, relations, consequences |
| Distributed work | physical constraints, role-specific knowledge, shared deliverable, consequences of mismatch |

These are not interchangeable kinds of resistance.

A mathematical counterexample does not correct in the same way a person saying “that is not what I meant” corrects.

A broken pump does not object in the way a promise constrains.

A text does not have an intention identical to its author's intention.

An aesthetic judgment is not settled by a meter merely because some of its features can be measured.

The technical gain is not to reduce these forms of answerability to one mechanism.

It is to see what they have in common at a higher level:

**the present understanding does not possess unilateral authority over what will count as faithful continuation.**

### 5. Answerability prevents purpose from becoming arbitrary

The book often asks:

**Enough for what?**

and:

**Same for what?**

Those questions introduce purpose.

They do not imply that a person can choose any purpose after the fact and thereby make a claim true.

The metro diagram is the same network map for route planning because the relations it preserves are genuinely relations in the transport system.

It is not an accurate walking-distance map merely because someone decides to use it that way.

The revised rowing procedure can plausibly count as continuing the old safety rule because it preserves and improves access to the information the rule existed to secure.

If the club replaced sign-out with a rule about shirt colour and called it the same safety practice, the assertion of continuity would lack answerability to the earlier purpose.

Purpose can therefore help specify the dimension of sameness without creating sameness from nothing.

### 6. Correction versus replacement

The hardest cases occur when transformation is extensive.

At what point is a corrected explanation a different explanation?

When does a translation become an invention?

When does a repaired staircase become another staircase?

When does a revised institutional practice become a new rule?

When Lia rewrites earlier chapters to make Mara's departure intelligible, is she discovering a possibility within the same character or creating another character?

The narrative does not provide a universal threshold.

That may not be a defect.

Different domains supply different continuity conditions.

Ownership, physical material, function, historical lineage, wording, intention, structural role and practical consequence can all matter differently.

A useful test is:

**Could the two versions come apart in a way that matters to the issue under discussion?**

If one promise could be kept while the other is broken, the paraphrase may have changed the undertaking.

If one translation preserves a contrast the other destroys, the difference may be substantive.

If one model predicts a result the other denies, they are not interchangeable for that prediction.

If one version of a character makes an earlier scene unintelligible, revision has crossed an important boundary even if the name remains the same.

This test does not solve identity.

It exposes where identity is doing work.

### 7. Preservation versus reconstruction

Another important distinction concerns time.

Something can remain continuously available.

Something can be forgotten and later recovered.

A record can survive while understanding disappears.

A later person can reconstruct an understanding from traces.

A skill can remain bodily available while the original learning episode is forgotten.

A question can survive in writing even when nobody currently feels its force.

These are different histories.

Saying simply that “the understanding survived” can conceal them.

The technical account therefore treats continuity as a relation among episodes rather than assuming an unbroken internal state.

### 8. Plural interpretations and open matters

Answerability does not guarantee uniqueness.

Celia's reading group finds that the story constrains interpretation without selecting one final reading.

Ravi and Mae become more precise about the jug while retaining intelligent disagreement.

Nessa and Daniel understand the moral conflict better without deriving one mechanically correct action.

These cases show that:

**constraint and plurality can coexist.**

That is important because otherwise answerability would merely be a new name for eventual convergence.

It is not.

A matter can resist some claims while leaving more than one live possibility.

### 9. Distributed achievement without a group mind

“No One Knows the Whole Thing” makes another extension necessary.

The exhibition is intelligible as one exhibition.

Its production depends on conservation, curation, translation, spatial design, access, safety and installation.

No participant contains the whole achievement mentally.

The technical account can say that the understanding is **socially constituted** in a modest sense:

different people's partial understandings become mutually relevant to the same ongoing work, and each can constrain what the others can reasonably do.

This does not require saying that the exhibition team forms one literal mind.

It does require rejecting the assumption that every intelligible human achievement must be fully represented inside one individual before it can exist as coordinated understanding.

This claim is **plausible and strongly supported**, but a full theory of collective cognition would go beyond what the narrative establishes.

### 10. Making Sense Together

The title can now be read more exactly.

“Together” does not mean agreement.

It does not mean identical representations.

It does not mean a mystical shared consciousness.

It includes:

- one person redirecting another's attention;
- one person noticing a difference another missed;
- teaching by demonstration;
- correction;
- disagreement about a shared matter;
- inherited results;
- inherited questions;
- records left for unknown later people;
- obligations carried across time;
- tacit coordination;
- partial mutual understanding;
- distributed work;
- people revising practices they did not create;
- later people taking up unfinished matters begun by earlier people.

Some human achievements are intelligible only because different people know different things that become mutually relevant to the same ongoing matter.

The strongest general claim the book has earned is therefore not that understanding is a private event later transmitted outward.

It is this:

**Human sense-making depends on forms of continuity that permit transformation while preserving answerability.**

Or, in less compressed language:

**What becomes intelligible can change form, person, medium and use, and still remain connected to what came before when later thought and action remain open to being corrected, supported, limited or redirected by the matter they claim to continue.**

That is not a final theory of understanding.

It is the map the thirty-seven chapters have earned.

| Successful coordination implies deep mutual understanding. | **Rejected** | The stagehands coordinate before discovering incompatible accounts of their cue. |
| Understanding can persist only through literal preservation. | **Rejected** | Translation, recipes, rules, restoration and the final drawer show transformation can carry continuity. |
| Same-matter continuity can often be tested by later bearing on earlier issues. | **Plausible heuristic** | Answering, contradicting, fulfilling, correcting and continuing are repeatedly useful tests. |
| Answerability is necessary for genuine continuity. | **Proposed** | It solves several problems but remains a theoretical commitment requiring further testing. |
| Different domains have different forms of answerability. | **Established here** | Physical, mathematical, interpersonal, normative, textual and institutional cases differ materially. |
| Some achievements can be socially constituted without a group mind. | **Plausible and strongly supported** | The exhibition is the clearest case. |
| The book supplies a complete theory of collective cognition. | **No** | That would exceed the evidence. |

## Appendix B: Operational Vocabulary and Minimal Notation

The following notation is a compression device. It is **operational**, not ontological. It should not be read as a claim that thought literally consists of discrete state vectors or that every episode has exact boundaries.

### B.1 Episode

Let an **episode** E be a bounded unit chosen for analysis.

The unit can be small:

- noticing the loose runner;
- getting a joke;
- seeing the counterexample.

Or extended:

- learning to ride;
- revising the rowing rule;
- producing the museum exhibition.

The choice of episode is analytical. A single larger episode can contain many smaller ones.

### B.2 Participants

Let P denote the relevant person or set of people.

P may be:

- one person;
- two people;
- a changing group;
- a set of participants with different roles.

Using P for a group does not imply one group mind.

### B.3 Situation

Let S denote the relevant situation.

S may contain:

- physical objects and conditions;
- words and records;
- mathematical relations;
- social expectations;
- promises;
- rules;
- texts;
- models;
- institutional arrangements;
- fictional structures;
- other people.

The situation includes only what matters to the analysis at hand. It is not a complete description of reality.

### B.4 Availability profile

Let A denote the set or pattern of things presently available to P in relation to S.

This can include what P can:

- distinguish;
- relate;
- anticipate;
- test;
- articulate;
- perform;
- rely on;
- question;
- revise;
- continue.

We can write a minimal episode as:

    <P, S, A0> -> <P, S', A1>

where A1 differs materially from A0.

S' may differ from S, as in a repaired drawer.

S' may be physically unchanged, as when Mara hears the same musical passage differently.

The notation therefore does not assume that understanding requires changing the external situation.

### B.5 Transformation

Let ΔA denote the relevant change from A0 to A1.

The candidate forms in Appendix C describe possible aspects of ΔA.

They are non-exclusive.

### B.6 Support

Let B denote the basis or support through which the change becomes warranted or stable enough to matter.

B may involve:

- one decisive feature;
- several features jointly;
- repeated testing;
- demonstration;
- proof;
- another person's correction;
- accumulated practice;
- some combination.

The current theory does not justify a complete finite list of support structures.

### B.7 Sufficiency

Where “enough” is relevant, define a sufficiency relation:

    Suff(A1 | C)

meaning:

    A1 is sufficient relative to context or demand C.

C may specify:

- purpose;
- risk;
- question;
- range;
- intended use;
- next action;
- standard of proof.

This notation is deliberately conditional.

It does not say that A1 is sufficient without qualification.

Some episodes do not have a natural terminal sufficiency judgment. In those cases the relation should not be forced.

### B.8 Remainder

Let R denote whatever from the episode can matter later.

R is not one kind of object.

It may be:

- a proposition;
- relation;
- skill;
- habit;
- memory;
- record;
- question;
- warning;
- obligation;
- model;
- practice;
- disposition;
- changed interpretation.

### B.9 Uptake

Let U denote a later uptake of R.

U may be:

- use;
- recall;
- reconstruction;
- teaching;
- translation;
- application;
- correction;
- challenge;
- modification;
- institutionalization.

A later episode Ej may therefore take up an earlier episode Ei:

    Ei --R/U--> Ej

This does not imply that the same mental content is copied from Ei into Ej.

### B.10 Continuity and answerability

Let Cont(Ei, Ej, M) be the claim that Ei and Ej concern the same continuing matter M in the respect under analysis.

Let Ans(Ej, M) mean that what happens in Ej remains answerable to M: there are standards, evidence, consequences, prior commitments, textual structures or other constraints capable of counting for or against the claimed continuation.

Then a proposed minimal condition is:

    Cont(Ei, Ej, M) requires Ans(Ej, M).

This is a **proposed necessary condition**, not a proven theorem.

It says that mere assertion of continuity is insufficient.

It does not say that answerability alone is sufficient to establish identity.

## Appendix C: The Three-Question Schema and the Candidate 54

### C.1 The three questions

The current working schema asks:

1. **What changed?**
2. **Why was that enough here?**
3. **What remained afterward?**

The first and third questions continue to work across most of the narrative.

The second requires qualification because some cases do not end in “enough,” and because its candidate answer categories mix different logical roles.

### C.2 Candidate forms of change

For compact notation:

| Code | Candidate description |
| --- | --- |
| C1 | A difference became salient or usable. |
| C2 | Something became possible or available. |
| C3 | A relation became intelligible. |
| C4 | A mismatch or failure was put right. |
| C5 | Several elements became organized into a functioning whole. |
| C6 | A live question received an answer or sufficient determination. |

Membership is non-exclusive.

### C.3 Candidate sufficiency forms

The original candidate list was:

| Code | Candidate description |
| --- | --- |
| S1 | One particular feature or consideration decided the matter. |
| S2 | Several features jointly provided sufficient support. |
| S3 | Something that had been controlling the next move stopped controlling it. |

The technical debrief records a structural objection:

- S1 and S2 describe **support distribution**.
- S3 describes a **practical effect** of the change.

Therefore the S dimension is not currently shown to be orthogonal.

### C.4 Candidate remainder forms

| Code | Candidate description |
| --- | --- |
| R1 | Something is established. |
| R2 | What remains is essentially relational. |
| R3 | What remains is mainly visible through what can now happen. |

Again, membership is non-exclusive.

A result can be established, relational and efficacious at once.

It is a combinatorial expansion of three answer-lists.

### C.6 What would be required to justify a finite 54-form system?

At minimum:

1. **Exhaustiveness**  
   There would need to be reason to believe each list captures all relevant forms at its level.

2. **Clear membership rules**  
   Analysts would need operational criteria for deciding when an episode belongs to a category.

3. **Treatment of overlap**  
   Either categories would need to be exclusive, or the theory would need explicit rules for multiple membership.

4. **Independence or controlled dependence**  
   The dimensions would need to cross in a principled way. If one category is an effect of another dimension, simple multiplication is misleading.

5. **Discriminative usefulness**  
   Different cells would need to make a meaningful analytical difference.

6. **Hard-case performance**  
   Music, humour, skill, grief, plural interpretation, moral conflict, translation, aesthetic judgment and distributed work would need to fit without being distorted.

7. **Reliability**  
   Different competent analysts should be able to apply the scheme with reasonable consistency.

The narrative establishes none of these strongly enough to call 54 an exhaustive ontology.

At present, the most defensible status is:

**54 is a disciplined prompt generated by a provisional crossing, not a discovered periodic table of understanding.**

## Appendix D: Answerability and Same-Matter Tests

The following tests are heuristics for analysing continuity. No one test is sufficient in every domain.

### D.1 Bearing test

Ask:

**Can the later event genuinely bear on the earlier issue?**

Can it:

- answer it;
- contradict it;
- correct it;
- fulfil it;
- test it;
- explain it;
- continue it;
- realize it;
- transform it?

If not, the connection may be resemblance rather than continuation.

### D.2 Separation test

Ask:

**Could the two formulations come apart?**

Could one promise be kept while the other is broken?

Could one model prediction be true while the other is false?

Could one translation preserve a contrast the other loses?

If yes, the difference is substantive for at least some purpose.

### D.3 Constraint test

Ask:

**What could count against the claim of continuity?**

If the answer is “nothing,” the continuity claim is too unconstrained.

Possible sources include:

- physical behaviour;
- measurement;
- proof;
- testimony;
- the other person's correction;
- established textual structure;
- prior undertaking;
- institutional purpose;
- practical consequence.

### D.4 Purpose test

Ask:

**Same for what?**

Specify the respect in which sameness matters.

Then ask:

**Does the stated purpose have a real connection to the matter, or has it been invented to save the claim?**

Purpose should clarify continuity, not manufacture it arbitrarily.

### D.5 Transformation ledger

Record what changed:

- wording;
- medium;
- person;
- physical material;
- implementation;
- context;
- use;
- representation;
- supporting evidence.

Then ask what, if anything, still carries bearing from the earlier occasion into the later one.

### D.6 Replacement test

Ask:

**What change would make us stop calling this a revision and start calling it a replacement?**

The answer may differ by domain.

The value of the test is often in exposing the relevant continuity conditions, not in finding one universal threshold.

## Appendix F: Counterexamples, Limiting Cases and Open Problems

### F.1 Changed availability without understanding

A person can become more likely to produce a response through repetition, conditioning, imitation or social pressure without understanding the matter.

This is the strongest reason not to define understanding simply as changed availability.

The proposed repair is answerability: the changed availability must be organized in a way that remains responsive to what the matter permits, requires or resists.

Whether that condition is sufficient remains open.

### F.2 Rote correctness

Correct answer, poor understanding.

Amira's early multiplication is the book's simplest case.

A formal theory should therefore avoid defining understanding by output correctness alone.

### F.3 Fluent explanation without practical understanding

The reverse is also possible.

A person can repeat an explanation, rule or recipe without being able to use it appropriately.

This pressures any account that treats verbal articulation as the central vehicle of understanding.

### F.4 Skilled performance and automaticity

Aisha can ride without knowing how to explain her outside-foot movement.

But automatic behaviour can also occur without what we would ordinarily call understanding.

The boundary between embodied understanding, trained automaticity and mere habit remains underdeveloped.

### F.5 Hallucination and dream

The narrative did not directly test hallucination or dream cases.

They matter because a person may experience strong internal intelligibility that is poorly answerable to shared evidence.

The present theory predicts that felt coherence alone is insufficient for robust understanding, but the book has not earned a detailed account.

This remains **open**.

### F.6 Fictional truth

Lia's character is invented, yet earlier characterization constrains later writing.

This supports a domain-relative form of answerability.

It does not by itself settle broader questions about fictional truth, authorial intention or reader interpretation.

### F.7 Aesthetic disagreement

Ravi and Mae show that criticism can become more exact without convergence.

The book therefore rejects both extremes:

- aesthetic judgment is mechanically decidable;
- aesthetic judgment is unconstrained preference.

A fuller account of aesthetic reasons remains open.

### F.8 Moral conflict

Nessa and Daniel understand the facts and obligations better while retaining a genuine conflict.

The framework can describe changed availability and answerability.

It does not supply a moral decision procedure.

That limitation should remain explicit.

### F.9 Distributed understanding

The exhibition suggests that an intelligible achievement can depend on different people holding different partial understandings.

Open questions include:

- when it is useful to attribute understanding to the group rather than the process;
- whether group-level understanding requires any participant to understand the integration;
- how errors propagate when no person can inspect the whole.

### F.10 Same matter across radical transformation

Translation, restoration, rule change and fiction all show continuity under transformation.

No universal numerical threshold separates revision from replacement.

A mature theory may require domain-specific continuity conditions rather than one cross-domain identity criterion.

### F.11 The three questions may not be exhaustive

“What changed?” remains robust.

“What remained?” remains productive if interpreted broadly.

“Why was that enough?” is less universal because some episodes do not end in enoughness and because the original support categories are structurally mixed.

The technical account should therefore retain the three questions as a powerful analytic device while allowing revision of the second.

## Appendix G: Propositions and Conjectures

The following list states the current theory as compactly as possible.

### Propositions strongly supported by the narrative

**P1.** Making sense is not identical with being correct.

**P2.** Agreement is neither necessary nor sufficient for shared engagement with a matter.

**P3.** Understanding need not be verbally articulable in full.

**P4.** Recognition and deeper intelligibility can arise without a prior consciously formulated problem.

**P5.** Understanding can improve while uncertainty remains.

**P6.** Earlier understanding can affect later action without the original episode being consciously recalled.

**P7.** Literal preservation is not required for continuity; translation, revision and reimplementation can preserve important relations.

**P8.** Correction need not erase everything in the earlier understanding.

**P9.** Different domains permit different things to count for or against an understanding.

**P10.** Successful coordination does not entail identical or deep mutual understanding.

### Technical proposals

**T1.** Changed availability is a recurring signature of understanding.

**T2.** Availability change should be supplemented by matter-relative answerability in any stronger definition.

**T3.** Sufficiency should be represented as conditional on a context, demand, purpose, risk or range.

**T4.** Same-matter continuity is usefully analysed through later bearing: answering, contradicting, correcting, fulfilling, testing, explaining, continuing, realizing or transforming what was earlier at issue.

**T5.** Answerability is a plausible necessary condition for non-arbitrary continuity.

**T6.** Socially constituted understanding can be analysed without positing a literal group mind.

### Conjectures and open questions

**C1.** Every important episode of human sense-making can be usefully analysed by some revised form of the three questions.

**C2.** The six candidate change descriptions can be refined into a stable finite system.

**C3.** A finite combinatorial taxonomy of understanding is possible.

**C4.** The original 6 × 3 × 3 crossing can be repaired into genuinely independent dimensions.

**C5.** Answerability plus changed availability is sufficient to distinguish understanding from all relevant neighbouring phenomena.

**C6.** One cross-domain account of same-matter continuity can do more work than domain-specific continuity criteria.

At present, none of C1–C6 should be treated as established.

## Appendix H: Scope Conditions

This framework concerns ordinary human sense-making across practical, mathematical, social, textual, normative and representational settings.

It does not, by itself, provide:

- a complete theory of consciousness;
- a theory of neural implementation;
- a full epistemology of knowledge and justification;
- a complete semantics of meaning;
- a moral theory;
- a theory of aesthetic value;
- a complete theory of collective cognition;
- an exhaustive ontology of social reality;
- a proof that the candidate categories are universal.

The framework is strongest when used to analyse how intelligibility changes, how that change becomes usable, what can remain afterward, and how later uptake stays answerable to what came before.

The theory should be judged by whether it clarifies those phenomena without flattening the cases that resist it.

If the formal map becomes cleaner by making the stories less intelligible, the map has failed.
