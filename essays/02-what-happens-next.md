## Chapter 2 — What Happens Next

A child finds the right key.

The door opens.

What happens next?

Usually, we stop paying attention to the key-finding event. The problem is over. The child walks through the door.

But that ordinary disappearance is exactly what matters.

Working something out changes what becomes possible afterward.

The child does not stand in front of the door and begin the same search again. The earlier work has altered the starting point. A question that was open is no longer open in the same way. Something has been carried forward.

That simple fact moves us beyond the fifty-four forms introduced in Chapter 1.

Those forms describe **how a local matter becomes enough**.

But life does not happen as a collection of isolated moments of enoughness.

One achievement becomes part of the conditions of another.

A route is cleared, so the bicycle can move.

A cable is identified, so the brake can be tested.

A theorem is proved, so another proof can begin from it.

A decision is made, so people can book tickets.

A child learns to read a word, so the next sentence becomes possible.

The important question now is:

> **What did working this out change about what could happen next?**

---

## What is left after a problem is solved?

Suppose you are fixing a bicycle.

At first, you do not know which cable controls the rear brake.

You pull one cable.

The rear brake moves.

Now you know.

A moment ago, the live question was:

> Which cable controls the rear brake?

Now it is not.

Something from that achievement remains.

Perhaps what remains is simply:

> **This cable is the rear-brake cable.**

That is already useful.

You can begin the next step from there.

But there is more than one way for something to remain.

Imagine two people who both know the correct cable.

The first remembers only which one it is.

The second remembers how they found out: pulling this cable produced movement here, while the other cable affected something else.

They possess the same result.

They do not possess the same path.

That difference may not matter when everything keeps working.

It may matter enormously when something changes.

---

## Result and path

Consider a simple piece of mathematics.

A student knows:

\[
7\times8=56.
\]

That answer can be used immediately.

If the next calculation is:

\[
7\times8+4,
\]

there is no need to rebuild the multiplication from first principles.

The result has become a new starting point.

That is one of the great efficiencies of intelligence.

But suppose the student is asked:

> Why is \(7\times8\) the same as eight groups of seven?

Or:

> How would you work out \(7\times18\) if you forgot the answer?

Or:

> Your classmate says \(7\times8=54\). How could you check?

Now the path matters.

An answer lets us continue quickly.

A path lets us rebuild, check, explain, teach, or repair.

We should therefore distinguish:

### The result

What can be carried forward as achieved.

### The path

Enough of how the result was reached to make the achievement reconstructible.

The word *path* should not be taken too literally.

A path might be:

- a proof;
- a set of dependencies;
- a repair sequence;
- a reason;
- a record of evidence;
- a demonstration;
- an apprenticeship;
- a causal explanation;
- a set of intermediate decisions.

It need not preserve every historical detail.

It preserves enough structure for somebody to work out why the result deserved to stand, or how to reach it again.

---

## Why we cannot keep every path

If every answer required us to replay its entire path every time we used it, almost nothing complex would be possible.

Imagine proving basic arithmetic before buying groceries.

Imagine rebuilding the science of electricity every time you switched on a light.

Imagine re-deriving every engineering standard before tightening a bolt.

Imagine learning your language from the beginning every morning.

We survive by **compressing earlier work**.

A long process becomes a short starting point.

A proof becomes a theorem we can cite.

A repair becomes a working bicycle.

A series of lessons becomes a skill.

A long discussion becomes a decision.

Years of experience can become a glance that tells an expert what matters.

Compression is not laziness.

It is one of the main ways earlier achievement becomes usable.

But compression always raises another question:

> **What did we leave out?**

Sometimes it does not matter.

Sometimes it does.

A person may remember the answer and forget why.

A company may keep the procedure and lose the people who understand it.

A culture may preserve a rule after forgetting the problem the rule originally solved.

A student may memorize a formula without knowing when it applies.

Compression gives speed.

Lost path creates vulnerability.

---

## Two people can arrive at the same place differently

Suppose two students both answer a problem correctly.

On the page, their final answers are identical.
One rebuilt the answer from a well-understood relation.

The other remembered it from a previous example.

Locally, the two events may have the same shape in the terms introduced in Chapter 1.

The same kind of change may have occurred.

The same thing may have counted as enough.

The same kind of result may remain.

Yet their futures can still differ.

Give them the exact same problem tomorrow and both may succeed.

Change the assumptions slightly and one may adapt while the other becomes stuck.

Ask them to teach somebody else and the difference may grow.

Ask them to decide whether the old answer still applies and it may grow again.

This tells us something important.

A local form does not contain the whole history of how it was reached.

Nor does it determine the whole future.

The fifty-four tell us something important about the moment a matter became enough.

They do not tell us everything that was carried forward from that moment.

The state after completion is richer than the coordinate alone.

We will return to that distinction many times.

---

## Sequence is not enough

Now imagine two events.

First, I identify the rear-brake cable.

Five seconds later, I notice that it is raining.

Both things happen in order.

But the first did not make the second possible.

The rain did not become visible because I identified the cable.

They are a sequence.

They are not yet the kind of connection we are looking for.

Now consider a different pair.

First, I identify the rear-brake cable.

Then I test its tension.

The second event depends on the first.

Without the earlier identification, I would not know which cable to test.

The earlier achievement changed:

- what I could sensibly do;
- what I could notice;
- what I could assume;
- what question could now become live;
- what would count as enough in the next step.

This is more than chronology.

Something from the first event has entered the second.

---

## When earlier work becomes part of the next problem

This pattern is everywhere.

A child learns that two written marks make different sounds.

That distinction changes what words can now be read.

A mechanic finds the faulty component.

That identification changes which repair becomes relevant.

A scientist establishes one relation.

That result becomes part of the starting conditions of a later experiment.

A group decides the date of an event.

That decision makes venue booking possible.

A programmer discovers where a bug begins.

That discovery changes which parts of the system now deserve inspection.

An earlier achievement matters because what remains from it changes the field of the later activity.

A simple test helps.

Ask:

> **If the earlier work had not been done, would the later matter be different?**

Would it be impossible?

Would it be harder?

Would different things seem relevant?

Would a different answer count as enough?

Would the next step have a different reason behind it?

If removing the earlier achievement changes the later field in one of these ways, then the connection is structural, not merely chronological.

---

## Traversal

We now have enough of the phenomenon to give it a compact name.

MST calls this a **traversal**.

A traversal occurs when something achieved earlier becomes part of the conditions from which a later matter can be worked out.

The important thing is not that one event happened before another.

The earlier event must make a difference to the later one.

In formal language, we can write:

\[
E_i \leadsto E_j
\]

when what remains from \(E_i\) materially changes the opening, the possible moves, the standard of enoughness, the warrant, or the achievable action in \(E_j\).

But the ordinary question remains more useful:

> **What can happen now because of what we worked out before?**

That is the heart of traversal.

---

## One achievement can change the next question

Sometimes the earlier achievement supplies an answer.

Sometimes it changes the question itself.

Imagine looking for a fault in a machine.

At first the live question is:

> Which system is failing?

You discover that the electrical system is working properly.

That result may not solve the original problem.

But it changes the field.

The next question might become:

> Is the failure mechanical?

The earlier completion has narrowed what now needs attention.

In another situation, an achievement may open possibilities instead of narrowing them.

A child learns to balance on a bicycle.

Suddenly steering, braking, riding on a path, and navigating traffic become new possible matters.

A new ability creates new openings.

Working something out therefore does not merely add facts to a store.

It can reorganize what becomes askable.

---

## What counts as enough can change too

Earlier work can alter not only the available question but also what would count as a satisfactory answer.

Suppose a doctor initially needs to decide whether an infection is likely.

A quick test may be enough for that decision.

Later, after the patient's condition changes, the question becomes which treatment will be safe given several interacting factors.

The earlier answer still matters.

But the later matter demands more.

A single result that was enough at one scale may become only one part of what must now come together.

This is another reason we cannot treat local completion as finality.

Enoughness belongs to a particular matter at a particular time and scale.

The next matter may inherit the result while asking for something more.

---

## A path can disappear while the result remains

A result often survives longer than its path.

You may remember:

> Take the second left after the bridge.

and forget why that was the quickest route.

You may know:

> Use this setting for the machine.

and have no idea which tests established it.

You may inherit:

> This is the accepted formula.

without knowing its derivation.

That can be perfectly efficient.

Until something changes.

Suppose the bridge closes.

Now the stored result cannot simply be repeated.

We need some way to work the matter out again.

If the old path has been preserved, it may help.

If not, we may have to rebuild from much further back.

This is why path matters even when it seems unnecessary during ordinary use.

A path is partly what makes reopening intelligent.

---

## When should we check again?

Imagine a family has found the quickest road to school.

For months, they use it without thinking.

The route has become compressed into habit.

Then one morning a bridge on the route is closed.

The old result was not bad.

It worked.

But something important has changed.

Now the question returns.

> **Can we still rely on this?**

This is not the same as asking whether we should distrust everything we learned before.

If we reopened every settled matter constantly, nothing would stay usable long enough for complex life.

But if we never reopened anything, yesterday's good answers would become today's rigid mistakes.

Intelligence requires both:

- the ability to continue from what has already been worked out;
- the ability to notice when the conditions for doing so have changed.

The second ability will become increasingly important later in the book.

For now, notice the basic rhythm:

> work something out;

> use what remains;

> check again when the world no longer fits what was inherited.

Reopening does not erase the earlier achievement.

It places it back inside a changed situation.

---

## Expertise hides its own paths

Watch an experienced mechanic listen to an engine.

A novice hears noise.

The expert may immediately attend to one part of the sound and begin checking a particular component.

From the outside, the expert seems to have skipped steps.

Often they have.

But not because no steps ever existed.

Earlier work has been compressed.

Repeated distinctions, repairs, comparisons, mistakes, and successful diagnoses have changed what the expert notices now.

The result of many earlier traversals is available almost at once.

This is why expertise can be fast without being shallow.

Historical work has become present structure.

But expertise creates a familiar difficulty.

An expert may know what to do and struggle to explain how they know.

The ability remains.

Part of the path may have disappeared from easy access.

Teaching then requires a kind of unpacking.

The teacher must make visible again enough of what expertise has compressed for another person to travel the route.

This will become central when we turn to learning and teaching.

---

## Reasoning happens between conclusions

We often imagine reasoning as a line of statements:

\[
A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D.
\]

But a list of individually understandable statements is not automatically good reasoning.

The important question lies in the arrows.

Why does \(A\) allow \(B\)?

What did \(B\) establish that made \(C\) legitimate?

Which dependency must survive for \(D\) to follow?

A proof is not merely a pile of true sentences.

It is an organized sequence in which earlier achievements change what can lawfully follow.

The same is true in:

- diagnosis;
- debugging;
- planning;
- repair;
- investigation;
- design;
- practical judgment.

Reasoning can fail even when every local sentence sounds sensible.

The failure may be between them.

A later move can be unearned.

Traversal gives us a way to ask where that happened.

---

## Not every possible next step is equally available

If the fifty-four local forms are embedded in longer activity, some transitions may be easier than others.

Telling two things apart can make it possible to connect them.

Discovering a relation can make it possible to put several parts together.

Fixing one fault can open a new test.

Dropping an obsolete constraint can make a previously blocked route available.

Learning a skill can create whole families of new matters.

These are not universal sequences.

There is no fixed ladder every mind must climb.

But there may be regularities in what kinds of achievements tend to prepare what kinds of later openings.

If such regularities exist, the real structure of MST will not be visible only in the fifty-four cells.

It will also be visible in the paths between them.

That structure cannot be read directly from the table.

Two cells that look close in the formal product may be very difficult to move between in practice.

Two cells that look distant may be crossed effortlessly by an expert.

The geometry of sense-making, if there is one, will have to be discovered from actual trajectories.

---

## The same local form can lead to different futures

Return to the two students who arrived at the same answer.

The coordinate of their local completion may be the same.

But one preserved the path and the other did not.

One may be able to teach.

One may be able to adapt after a changed assumption.

One may know when the old result stops applying.

One may simply continue repeating it.

This tells us why the fifty-four cannot be the whole theory.

Their local precision matters precisely because they can now be placed inside something larger.

They tell us:

> How did this matter become enough?

The larger movement asks:

> What from that achievement survived strongly enough to alter the future?

And then:

> How?

---

## What remains does not have to be inside a person

So far, many examples have sounded psychological.

Someone remembers a result.

Someone keeps a skill.

Someone understands a relation.

But what remains can also sit outside any individual.

A repaired machine remains repaired.

A note remains on a desk.

A proof remains in a book.

A road sign remains beside the road.

A tool remains shaped in a way that guides future action.

A decision remains in meeting minutes.

A computer file remains available to someone who was not present when it was created.

A result can therefore outlive the person who achieved it.

A path can too.

This opens a much larger question.

How can what one person has worked out become something another person can begin from?

The answer cannot simply be:

> Put the understanding into words.

Words do not contain another person's understanding ready-made.

A listener still has to do something.

A reader still has to rebuild enough structure for the result to become usable again.

So the next chapter begins with a familiar miracle:

One person speaks.

Another person hears.

Nothing material resembling the first person's understanding travels between their heads.

And yet, sometimes, the second person can now see, know, decide, or do something they could not before.

How?

That is the next problem.

## Continuity note

Chapter 3 should begin outside linguistics with the practical problem of **one person's achieved organization becoming usable by another**. The public claim should be: **words do not contain understanding; they help another person build it**. Preserve the stronger claim that language is a realization system over an architecture larger than language. Carry forward result/path, compression, traversal, and reopening, but do not yet disclose the full “architecture of inheritance” synthesis.
