# Chapter 5 — Growing Into Sense

A baby reaches for a cup and misses.

The cup is too far away.

The baby leans farther.

Still too far.

An adult moves the cup closer.

Now the baby grasps it.

Nothing in this small event requires a sentence.

There is a difference between reachable and unreachable. There is an expectation. There is failure. There is change. There is success. And what happens now can affect what the child tries next.

Long before children can explain what they are doing, they are already living inside a world in which some things work, some do not, some differences matter, some actions open possibilities, and some results can be used again.

That is where development begins for this book.

Not with words.

With a history already moving.

## Before words

It would be easy to imagine that sense-making begins when a child acquires names.

The world is there. Language arrives. Then the child begins to organize it.

But ordinary development suggests something more complicated.

A child can:

- distinguish a familiar face from an unfamiliar one;
- anticipate what happens when an object is dropped;
- learn that one action makes a toy respond;
- notice when a familiar pattern changes;
- reach differently after repeated failure;
- retain an expectation;
- generalize from one episode to another.

None of this establishes the full architecture proposed in this book.

It does not prove that every one of the six ways of changing, three ways of becoming enough, and three ways of keeping is already present before speech.

That remains an empirical question.

But it gives us a strong starting point:

> **Language does not enter an empty field.**

It enters a life in which differences, actions, failures, successes, relations, and expectations are already being organized.

The child is not waiting for language before anything can become enough.

## A toy before a word

Imagine a simple toy with two large buttons.

Pressing the blue button makes a light flash.

Pressing the yellow button does nothing.

At first the child presses both.

Then, gradually, behaviour changes.

The blue button is approached more often.

Something has been learned.

We can ask the same ordinary questions we used before.

What was being worked out?

Which action changes the toy?

What changed?

A useful difference became operative.

What made it enough?

Perhaps repeated success with one button became sufficient.

What remained?

An expectation, a preference, or a usable ability.

The child need not formulate any of this.

The architecture concerns what the event does, not whether the child has a theory of the event.

> **Instantiating a structure is not the same as being able to describe the structure.**

We use grammar long before we can explain grammar.

We balance our bodies long before we can explain balance.

The same may be true of the structures by which local matters become enough.

## Learning changes the next opening

Development is not simply the accumulation of more contents.

Each achievement changes the field from which later achievements begin.

A child who has learned that objects continue to exist when hidden approaches hiding differently.

A child who can crawl has access to different problems from one who cannot.

A child who can point can recruit another person into a task.

A child who can name a missing object can open a problem socially that previously had to be solved through searching alone.

A child who can read one word can enter a sentence that was previously unavailable.

Learning therefore does more than fill an internal store.

It changes what can become open next.

This is the developmental importance of traversal.

Earlier achievements restructure later beginnings.

The child's present is full of compressed history.

## First language does not merely label a finished world

If language enters an already moving architecture, first-language learning cannot simply be the attachment of words to concepts that are completely formed in advance.

Language changes the activity itself.

A child who can say:

> “Again”

can preserve a demand across time.

A child who can ask:

> “Why?”

can keep a relation open instead of accepting a result.

A child who learns:

> “Yesterday”

and:

> “tomorrow”

can organize events that are not present.

A child who learns:

> “because”

gains a public way to ask for and offer a path rather than only an answer.

A child who learns:

> “maybe”

can keep settlement from becoming stronger than the evidence supports.

Language therefore does not simply name distinctions the child already possesses in full.

It reorganizes which distinctions can be made, shared, revisited, combined, and carried forward.

First-language development is a process in which ways of making sense and ways of realizing them grow together.

## Language makes other people's paths available

Without language, a child can learn from demonstration, imitation, repetition, correction, and participation.

With language, another possibility expands dramatically.

Someone can preserve part of a path the child did not personally travel.

A parent can say:

> “Don't touch that pan. It's hot.”

The child need not discover the result through injury.

A teacher can explain a relationship that took earlier generations centuries to establish.

A story can preserve the consequences of choices the listener has never made.

A warning can alter action before failure occurs.

A proof can make a dependency reconstructible without the learner having to rediscover it unaided.

Language therefore changes development by changing what can be inherited.

A child can begin from achievements whose original paths happened elsewhere.

This does not eliminate the need to reconstruct.

It changes the starting point.

## Second language is a different problem

Now consider an adult learning another language.

They are not beginning in the same way.

They already know how to:

- request;
- refuse;
- explain;
- compare;
- warn;
- promise;
- tell stories;
- distinguish certainty from doubt;
- ask for reasons;
- give instructions;
- repair misunderstandings.

The learner may already possess many of the structures they want the new language to realize.

The problem is often:

> **How does this language let me do what I already know how to do?**

That is not the whole of second-language learning.

A new language can introduce unfamiliar distinctions, new cultural expectations, new conventional ways of organizing attention, and new patterns of interaction.

But the starting condition is different.

The learner is often remapping an already rich history into another realization system.

This helps explain why a learner can know exactly what they mean and still not know how to say it.

The underlying practical achievement is available.

The new realization is not.

## Why transfer can help and mislead

Existing knowledge can make second-language learning easier.

It can also create predictable errors.

If one language usually leaves a distinction to context while another requires it to be marked, a learner may repeatedly omit something the new language expects.

If one language uses word order to do work another language performs through endings, the learner may carry an old solution into a new system.

The mistake is not random.

The learner is trying to realize familiar achievements through an inherited set of resources.

Sometimes those resources transfer well.

Sometimes they do not.

This suggests a useful research question for MST:

> **Can some learning difficulties be predicted by the distance between two ways of realizing the same structural achievement?**

That is a hypothesis.

It should compete with ordinary linguistic explanations rather than replace them by declaration.

## Children make the hidden work visible

Adult competence is difficult to study because so much earlier work has become compressed.

We read words without consciously rebuilding letter-sound relations.

We catch implications without replaying the history that made them familiar.

We perform practiced actions while forgetting the route by which they became easy.

Children are useful because more of the construction remains visible.

A child learning to sort objects may visibly move from:

> “These look alike”

to:

> “This difference matters.”

A child learning a route may first follow landmarks one by one, then later seem simply to “know the way.”

A child learning arithmetic may count every item before later treating a result as something that stands.

Development lets us watch paths become compressed into starting points.

That is not a deficiency of childhood.

It is one of the clearest windows into how mature ease is built.

## Teaching changes what survives

Suppose two children both solve the same puzzle.

One is immediately told the next answer.

The other is asked:

> “How did you know?”

That question may change what survives.

The first child may keep the result.

The second may preserve more of the path.

Or the question may impose unnecessary verbal burden and interfere with a skill that is still becoming stable.

There is no universal rule that more explanation is always better.

The question is functional:

> **What will this learner need later?**

If the future demands routine performance, ability may matter most.

If it demands transfer, repair, or teaching, path preservation may become more important.

If it demands rapid recall, a stable result may be enough.

Development therefore connects naturally to the third local question:

> **What do we need to keep?**

That question now becomes the subject of the next chapter.

## Growing into a world already worked out

A child does not only accumulate personal achievements.

The child grows into an environment full of earlier ones.

Doors have handles.

Numbers have symbols.

Roads have signs.

Games have rules.

Tools embody solutions.

Adults carry expectations.

Books wait on shelves.

The world has already been organized by other people's completions.

Development therefore has two histories at once.

There is the child's own sequence of achievements.

And there is the inherited field into which those achievements enter.

The two become increasingly entangled.

Eventually it becomes hard to tell where personal discovery ends and inherited organization begins.

That is not a flaw.

It is what human development is like.

By adulthood, a person may carry thousands of answers, relations, and abilities whose original paths are partly or completely invisible.

Which brings us to a more precise question.

When something has become enough, what can still be there tomorrow?
