# Chapter 10 — Designing for Enough

A teacher decides whether to give another example.

A mechanic decides whether the repair has been tested enough.

A scientist decides whether evidence supports a claim strongly enough to publish.

A meeting chair decides whether discussion is finished enough to make a decision.

A translator decides whether the target text preserves enough for the reader to continue correctly.

A programmer decides whether a test suite has covered enough.

These people are already designing conditions of enoughness.

They may not use that phrase.

They do not need to.

The structure is present in the work.

What changes if we make it explicit?

## Six questions

The practical architecture of this book can be carried by six questions.

> **1. What are we trying to work out?**

> **2. What needs to change?**

> **3. How will we know when it is enough?**

> **4. What do we need to keep?**

> **5. What will this let us do next?**

> **6. When should we check it again?**

These questions are deliberately ordinary.

They can be used by a child.

They can also support formal specification.

Their power lies in sequence.

Together, they prevent several common failures.

## What are we trying to work out?

Many bad processes begin by solving the wrong problem well.

A meeting talks for an hour without agreeing what is actually open.

A student answers the question they expected rather than the one asked.

An AI system produces an excellent explanation of the wrong issue.

The first engineering task is therefore event definition.

What is live?

At what scale?

For whom?

Relative to what purpose?

If this is vague, “enough” becomes impossible to specify.

## What needs to change?

Once the matter is clear, the process should identify the kind of change required.

Do we need to:

- tell two things apart?
- open a blocked possibility?
- connect elements?
- fix a fault?
- put parts together?
- settle a question?

These correspond to the six current Transformation values.

The practical wording should usually come first.

The formal category can follow when useful.

A good intervention changes what the actual opening requires.

## How will we know when it is enough?

This is the stopping problem.

Will one decisive thing settle the matter?

Must several conditions come together?

Does success require something that currently blocks progress to stop governing?

Those are the public forms of:

- Focal;
- Distributed;
- Release.

Making this explicit can prevent both premature stopping and endless continuation.

A meeting should not keep talking merely because more can be said.

A safety check should not stop merely because one reassuring sign appears.

The topology of enoughness matters.

## What do we need to keep?

Not every completion needs to leave the same thing behind.

Sometimes the future needs:

- a result;
- a relation;
- an ability;
- a recoverable path;
- a boundary condition;
- some combination.

A document meant only to record a decision may not need the full path.

A scientific claim may.

A training programme may need to produce Efficacy rather than verbal recall.

A safety procedure may need the result and the condition that makes it valid.

This question turns persistence into a design variable.

## What will this let us do next?

Completion is not useful merely because something stopped.

Its importance is what it enables.

A well-designed completion makes the intended continuation possible.

So before settling something, ask:

> What should become possible afterward?

If the answer is unclear, the completion criterion may be poorly chosen.

This is particularly useful in education.

The target should not be:

> “The learner understood.”

It should be something observable:

> “The learner can now solve a new case.”

> “The learner can explain the relation.”

> “The learner can perform the procedure.”

> “The learner knows when to check the rule again.”

## When should we check it again?

Every result has a future.

Some are stable for a very long time.

Some depend on conditions that change quickly.

A good design therefore records not only:

> What have we decided?

but also:

> **What would make this decision need to become a question again?**

This single question can make institutions less brittle.

It can also make personal reasoning more intelligent.

Reopening becomes part of the design rather than an embarrassment after failure.

## A completion record

The simplest practical tool suggested by the architecture is a one-page **completion record**.

It asks:

### What is still open?

What exactly remains unresolved, broken, uncertain, unavailable, or undecided?

### What must change?

What difference has to occur?

### What would make that change enough?

What observable condition would let us continue?

### What must remain afterward?

Do we need an answer, a relation, an ability, a path, or several of these?

### What result should be preserved?

What may later be taken as established?

### What path should remain recoverable?

What would someone need in order to check, teach, rebuild, or repair this later?

### When would this no longer hold?

What changed condition should trigger checking again?

### What should this make possible next?

What action, inference, decision, or new problem should now become available?
This is not a new bureaucracy.

It should be used only where making the structure explicit improves the work.

The tool itself has to earn its place.

## Teaching

A teacher can use the six questions to design a lesson.

What is the learner trying to work out?

What change should occur?

What would count as enough?

What needs to remain tomorrow?

What should the learner be able to do next?

What changed circumstance should reveal whether the learner really understood the relation rather than memorized the answer?

This produces testable design.

## Meetings

Meetings often fail because they mix openings.

One person thinks the task is to explore.

Another thinks it is to decide.

Another thinks the decision was already made and the task is implementation.

The first question can expose this.

The third question can define when discussion is enough.

The fourth determines what should be recorded.

The sixth records conditions for reopening.

A decision can then become a usable starting point instead of a vague memory of conversation.

## Documents

A document can be designed around future reconstruction.

Who will use it?

What will they need to do?

What path must remain visible?

What context will they lack?

What assumptions should be recorded because future conditions may change?

This shifts documentation from:

> “Did we write it down?”

to:

> **“Can the future reader reconstruct what they need?”**

## Translation

Translation can use the same structure.

What is the source doing?

What must change in the target reader?

What counts as enough?

What must survive—result, relation, action, path, condition?

What should the target reader be able to do next?

Which contextual change would make the translation misleading?

This makes structural preservation practical.

## AI

AI systems can expose the same six questions internally or to users.

Not every response needs a visible checklist.

But high-consequence uses may benefit from explicit structure.

What is the live matter?

What evidence would be enough?

Which conditions are still unverified?

What should remain after the response?

What downstream action is being enabled?

What would make the answer need rechecking?

This does not solve trust automatically.

It gives trust something inspectable to attach to.

## Measurement

A framework becomes scientific when its distinctions support predictions that can fail.

The 54 should therefore not be protected by retrospective classification.

Experiments should specify a target form before participants encounter the task.

They should manipulate one axis while holding others as constant as possible.

They should test whether different forms produce different downstream consequences.

The larger dynamics should also be tested.

Does preserving a path improve adaptation when conditions change?

Does preserving when a rule holds improve calibrated reopening?

Does removing an earlier result alter a supposedly dependent later event?

Does structural preservation predict downstream action beyond surface similarity?

If not, the theory should change.

## Engineering the loop, not the cell

The 54 describe the local shape of becoming enough.

Engineering concerns a larger loop:

\[
\text{open}
\rightarrow
\text{change}
\rightarrow
\text{enough}
\rightarrow
\text{what remains}
\rightarrow
\text{what becomes possible next}
\rightarrow
\text{check again when needed}.
\]

The cell matters.

But the cell is one moment inside the process.

A designed system must care about the whole cycle.

## What has become visible

At the beginning of this book, the central puzzle was local.

There seemed to be recurring ways in which something became enough.

Then each chapter pushed outward.

Enoughness left something behind.

What remained changed what could happen next.

Language helped one person rebuild another person's achievement.

Translation showed that structure could survive a change of realization.

Development showed that every present opening has a history.

Persistence showed that answers, relations, and abilities can survive differently.

Writing allowed achievements to outlive their originators.

Institutions organized inherited starting points.

AI exposed the danger of answers that look complete before enoughness has been earned.

Engineering made the full process inspectable.

We can now finally ask:

> **What have we actually been looking at all along?**

That question belongs to the final chapter.

## Continuity note

Chapter 11 must now state the whole loop explicitly and make “architecture of inheritance” feel earned. It should reveal that the 54 are the local morphology of enoughness, while the larger theory concerns how achieved organization becomes a condition of future sense-making. The mature new centre is conditional inheritance: what remains may guide continuation only while the conditions that made it good enough still hold.
