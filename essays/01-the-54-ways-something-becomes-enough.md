# Chapter 1 — The 54: Ways Something Becomes Enough

A child stands in front of a door with three keys.

At first, any of them might be the right one.

She tries the first. Nothing.

She tries the second. It turns.

Something has changed.

A few minutes later, a bicycle is stuck behind a pile of boxes. One box is moved. Then another. The bicycle still cannot pass. Another box is shifted and, suddenly, there is a route all the way through.

Something has changed there too.

Elsewhere, someone hears a strange sound from a machine and discovers that one loose part is causing it. The part is tightened. The sound stops.

Someone else has five pieces of a shelf spread across the floor. Each piece is fine on its own, but they are not yet a shelf. The pieces are fitted together. Now they hold weight.

A group has been discussing where to meet. Several possibilities remain live until somebody notices that only one place is open at the required time. The discussion stops. They have an answer.

These events are very different.

Finding the right key is not the same activity as clearing a route, repairing a machine, assembling a shelf, or settling a decision.

Yet they share something.

Before each event, something was still open.

After it, something had become enough.

That small transition is the starting point of this book.

## Not every change is enough

The difference between **change** and **enough** is easy to miss because successful activity usually moves through it quickly.

Suppose a bicycle cannot pass through a crowded shed.

Moving the first box changes the situation.

Moving the second changes it again.

Perhaps the available space doubles.

But if the handlebars still cannot pass, the change is not yet enough for the matter at hand.

The important question is not:

> Did anything improve?

It is:

> **Is there now a usable route?**

Only when the answer becomes yes does this particular problem stop governing what happens.

The bicycle can move.

That means “enough” is always enough **for something**.

A doorway can be wide enough for a child and too narrow for a piano.

A medical explanation can be enough for a patient to follow an instruction and not enough for a surgeon to perform an operation.

A sketch can be enough to locate a café and not enough to build the street.

Nothing is simply enough on its own.

There is always some matter we are trying to work out.

That gives us our first question:

> **What are we trying to work out?**

Until that is clear, we cannot know what would count as enough.

## Three questions inside the moment

Now look again at the examples.

The child finds the right key.

The route becomes passable.

The machine is fixed.

The shelf comes together.

The meeting place is decided.

For each event we can ask three different questions.

> **What changed?**

> **What made the change enough?**

> **What remained?**

These questions sound almost too ordinary to carry much theory.

That is part of their importance.

They pick apart three things that successful sense-making often folds together.

Something becomes different.

That difference reaches a point where the current matter can stop demanding further work.

And something about the achievement is still available afterward.

The proposal developed in this book begins from the possibility that these three questions expose a recurrent structure in how local matters become enough.

The current architecture has six recurring answers to the first question, three to the second, and three to the third.

Together they give:

\[
6\times3\times3=54.
\]

The multiplication is simple.

What matters is what is being multiplied.

# What changed?

There appear to be at least six recurring ways in which an open matter becomes otherwise.

Their ordinary forms are:

- **tell apart**
- **open up**
- **connect**
- **fix**
- **put together**
- **settle**

The more formal names are:

- Differentiation
- Opening
- Relating
- Repair
- Integration
- Settlement

The plain phrases come first because the distinctions can be seen before they are named.

## Tell apart

Two things that could not usefully be distinguished become distinguishable in the way that matters.

Two screws look interchangeable until you notice that one has a different thread.

Two birds look alike until a marking becomes visible.

Two possible causes of a fault seem equally plausible until one test separates them.

The important change is not simply that more information appeared.

A **difference became usable**.

MST calls this **Differentiation**.

## Open up

Something unavailable becomes available.

A blocked passage clears.

A locked container opens.

A possible move becomes possible.

A line of inquiry that seemed stuck gains somewhere to go.

The defining change is an increase in reachable possibility.

MST calls this **Opening**.

We will sometimes need to distinguish this kind of change from the broader idea that a matter is initially *open*. Context will usually make the difference clear.

## Connect

A relation becomes operative.

You learn that this switch controls that light.

You see that one symptom follows from one failing component.

You discover that two quantities change together.

Two events that seemed separate turn out to depend on one another.

The change is not merely that two things are now present in thought.

Their **bearing on one another** has become part of the situation.

MST calls this **Relating**.

## Fix

Something wrong ceases to control what happens.

A wheel stops rubbing.

A calculation error is corrected.

A mistaken assumption is removed.

A badly fitted part is put right.

Something could change during a repair without the repair succeeding. The relevant change occurs when the fault no longer governs the result.

MST calls this **Repair**.

## Put together

Parts acquire places within a functioning whole.

Pieces become a shelf.

Components become a circuit.

Separate observations become one account.

People with different jobs become an operating team.

The parts need not lose their individuality.

What changes is that they become organized as parts of something larger.

MST calls this **Integration**.

## Settle

A demand for further answer comes to rest.

The group decides.

The diagnosis is reached.

The missing number is found.

The question no longer needs to remain live for the present purpose.

Settlement does not mean that the answer is eternally certain.

It means that, here and now, the current demand for further resolution has stopped governing the situation.

MST calls this **Settlement**.

These six forms answer only the first question.

They tell us **what kind of change occurred**.

They do not yet tell us why that change counted as enough.

# What made it enough?

Consider two investigations.

In the first, a detective needs to know which of four identical-looking keys opens a box. One key turns the lock.

That one observation may be enough.

In the second, an engineer wants to know whether an aircraft is ready to fly. No single successful check is enough. Fuel, controls, instruments, doors, weight, weather, and many other conditions may need to come together.

Both matters can settle.

But they become enough differently.

The present architecture distinguishes three recurring patterns.

## One thing was enough

Sometimes sufficiency gathers around one decisive point.

One key opens the lock.

One measurement crosses the required threshold.

One broken wire is found.

One final signature authorizes the process.

Other things may have happened beforehand, but one locus carries the decisive burden.

MST calls this **Focal** completion.

## Several things together were enough

Sometimes no single part can finish the matter.

Every section of a route must connect.

Several symptoms together support the diagnosis.

Every component must be present before the device works.

A proof requires a span of dependent steps.

The sufficiency lies in the organized plurality.

MST calls this **Distributed** completion.

## Something stopped getting in the way

Sometimes the event becomes enough not because another positive piece is added, but because an earlier restriction stops governing the situation.

The jammed wheel is freed.

The obstacle is removed.

A false requirement is dropped.

A dependency ends.

A person discovers that a rule they thought was binding does not apply here.

MST calls this **Release**.

These three patterns answer:

> **How will we know when the change is enough?**

That question is different from:

> What changed?

A repair may become enough because one decisive fault was corrected.

Another repair may require several faults to be corrected together.

A third may succeed because a constraint was removed.

The same broad kind of change can therefore reach enoughness in different ways.

# What remained?

Now imagine three students learning the same piece of mathematics.

The first can state the answer correctly tomorrow.

The second understands the relation that makes the answer true and can use that relation in a new problem.

The third has practiced until the procedure has become something they can reliably do.

All three learned something.

But what remains from the learning is different.

The current architecture distinguishes three broad forms.

## The answer remains

Something can now be taken as established.

> That is the correct key.

> The answer is twelve.

> This component is faulty.

> We are meeting at six.

There is now a determination from which later activity can proceed.

MST calls this **Standing**.

## The connection remains

Sometimes what persists is not adequately described as a bare answer.

A relation continues to organize what happens.

You know not only that the brake failed, but how the cable, lever, and brake relate.

You know why changing one variable changes another.

The achievement remains through a continuing structure of dependence, comparison, proportion, source, or relation.

MST calls this **Bearing**.

The word is compact but not immediately transparent. In ordinary language, it is often enough to ask:

> **Did we keep the connection?**

## The ability remains

Sometimes what persists is something the person or system can now do.

Ride the bicycle.

Tie the knot.

Use the tool.

Apply the procedure.

Continue the calculation.

The achievement survives as changed capacity.

MST calls this **Efficacy**.

In ordinary language:

> **Did we keep the ability?**

Again, these are not three amounts of the same thing.

Someone can remember an answer without being able to use it.

Someone can understand a relation without being able to perform the relevant action.

Someone can perform expertly while struggling to explain what they know.

That separation will matter later.

For now, it gives us the third question:

> **What do we need to keep?**

# Fifty-four ways

We can now see the structure.

For any local event, ask:

### What changed?

One of six currently proposed forms:

\[
6
\]

### What made it enough?

One of three currently proposed forms:

\[
3
\]

### What remained?

One of three currently proposed forms:

\[
3
\]

Their combinations give:

\[
6\times3\times3=54.
\]

Each combination specifies one candidate way in which a local matter can become enough.

Take an ordinary example.

A child is trying to identify which cable operates the rear brake of a bicycle.

She pulls one cable.

The rear brake moves.

What happened?

**What changed?**

A previously uncertain distinction became usable.

This is **tell apart — Differentiation**.

**What made it enough?**

One cable produced the decisive movement.

**One thing was enough — Focal**.

**What remained?**

The result now stands:

> this is the rear-brake cable.

**The answer remained — Standing**.

That gives one combination:

\[
\text{Differentiation}
\times
\text{Focal}
\times
\text{Standing}.
\]

Now change the situation.

Suppose the child is trying to understand how the entire brake system works.

One moving cable is no longer enough.

The lever, cable, caliper, pads, rim, and their relations must be understood together.

What becomes enough may now be:

\[
\text{Relating}
\times
\text{Distributed}
\times
\text{Bearing}.
\]

Same bicycle.

Different matter.

Different completion.

This is why the 54 do not belong to objects.

A bicycle is not a Repair.

A sentence is not a Settlement.

A diagram is not a Relating.

The form belongs to the **event**: what was open, what changed, what made that change enough, and what kind of achievement remained.

# The sentence is not the form

This matters especially when we look at language.

Consider:

\[
A=C.
\]

The marks on the page can remain exactly the same.

At one moment, \(A=C\) may be the thing we are trying to prove.

Later it may be the result we have established.

Later still it may become a premise we simply use while proving something else.

The inscription did not change.

Its place in the activity did.

Conversely, very different expressions can do structurally similar work.

A spoken explanation, a diagram, a gesture, an equation, and a physical demonstration may look nothing alike while allowing the same local matter to become enough in the same broad way.

This gives the 54 a larger possible significance.

They are not proposed as:

- fifty-four sentence types;
- fifty-four meanings;
- fifty-four topics;
- fifty-four objects people think about;
- fifty-four pieces stored somewhere in the mind.

They are proposed as recurring **forms of local enoughness**.

The carrier can change.

The topic can change.

The language can change.

The mechanism may change.

What may remain comparable is the organization by which this particular matter became enough.

# A local law of enoughness

We can now introduce a stronger phrase.

A form may be understood as a **local law of enoughness**.

The phrase does not mean a law imposed from outside.

It means that each form states constraints on what must have occurred for this kind of local completion to count as this kind of completion.

If we say an event involved:

\[
\text{Relating}
\times
\text{Distributed}
\times
\text{Bearing},
\]

we are making a structured claim.

We are saying:

- what changed was relational organization;
- the change became enough only through several things taken together;
- and what remained was a continuing relation that still organizes what follows.

That is more than attaching a label afterward.

It says what would have to be true of the event for the description to fit.

This is why the 54 matter only if they eventually make predictions.

If two forms that the theory distinguishes repeatedly behave no differently under careful testing, the distinction should be questioned.

If the three questions collapse into one underlying factor, the architecture should change.

If completed events repeatedly appear that cannot be described without adding further basic distinctions, the architecture should change.

The number 54 is not sacred.

It is the current formal proposal.

What matters more deeply is the factorization:

> **What changed?**

> **What made it enough?**

> **What remained?**

The present claim is that these questions cut the local event at real joints rather than merely giving us three convenient ways to talk.

That claim now has to earn itself experimentally.

# Form is not the whole event

There is another limit we should establish immediately.

Knowing a coordinate does not tell us everything about what happened.

Two people may arrive at the same answer through radically different routes.

One may be certain, another hesitant.

One may remember every step, another only the result.

One may have struggled for an hour, another noticed the answer instantly.

One may be able to teach what happened, another may only be able to act.

The 54 do not attempt to encode all of this.

They describe the **local shape of becoming enough**.

They do not by themselves encode:

- the whole history that led there;
- how difficult the achievement was;
- who achieved it;
- whether the route can later be reconstructed;
- how confident anyone is;
- whether the result is true;
- whether it will remain useful forever;
- or everything it will make possible afterward.

That restraint matters.

A useful structure can become useless if it is forced to explain everything.

The 54 occupy one particular place.

They describe the moment in which an open matter becomes locally sufficient in a particular way and leaves something available afterward.

That is already a large claim.

It does not need to become a total theory of mind in order to matter.

# Enough does not mean true

Suppose someone confidently settles on the wrong diagnosis.

The matter may become closed for them.

An answer may stand.

Action may proceed from it.

Yet the answer can still be false.

Enoughness is therefore not truth.

Nor is it certainty.

Nor agreement.

Nor finality.

A group can agree and be wrong.

A scientist can proceed from a well-supported conclusion while remaining open to revision.

A person can reach a perfectly adequate answer for the present purpose and discover tomorrow that the situation has changed.

This is not a defect in the idea of enoughness.

It is what makes enoughness useful.

Human life would be impossible if every local achievement had to be final in all respects before anything else could happen.

We settle enough to continue.

Later, we may need to look again.

That possibility will become increasingly important.

# One event can sit inside another

There is also no rule that says only one matter can be open at a time.

A child may identify one piece while still assembling the whole object.

One sentence may settle while the argument remains open.

A diagnosis may settle while treatment remains unresolved.

A meeting may settle a date while leaving the budget undecided.

Local enoughness therefore depends on scale.

The question:

> Has this become enough?

always needs an implicit companion:

> **Enough for what, at what level?**

This prevents a common mistake.

A local settlement need not be the end of the larger activity.

Often it is precisely what lets the larger activity continue.

The correct screw is identified so that assembly can proceed.

The route is cleared so that the bicycle can move.

The theorem is established so that another proof can use it.

The meeting place is settled so that travel can be arranged.

A completion can close one matter while opening the practical possibility of another.

That observation pushes the 54 beyond a static table.

If working something out changes where we can begin next, the forms cannot remain isolated.

The next question is no longer merely:

> **What kind of completion was this?**

It is:

> **What did completing it change about what could happen next?**

That is where we turn now.

## Continuity note

Chapter 2 must begin from the last question rather than re-explaining the 54. It should introduce **result and path** early, show that chronological sequence is not enough, and let the idea later called **traversal** emerge from ordinary cases where what was worked out before materially changes the next opening. Reopening should appear as normal revisability, but the full “architecture of inheritance” synthesis must remain unnamed.
