# Chapter 5 — Growing Into Sense

A baby reaches for a cup and misses.

The cup is too far away.

The baby leans farther.

Still too far.

An adult moves the cup closer.

Now the baby grasps it.

Nothing in this small event requires a sentence.

There is a difference between reachable and unreachable. There is an expectation. There is failure. There is change. There is success. And what happens now can affect what the child tries next.

Long before children can explain what they are doing, they are already living inside a world in which some things work, some do not, some differences matter, some actions open possibilities, and some results can be used again.

That is where development begins for this book.

Not with words.

With a history already moving.

## Before words

It would be easy to imagine that sense-making begins when a child acquires names.

The world is there. Language arrives. Then the child begins to organize it.

But ordinary development suggests something more complicated.

A child can:

- distinguish a familiar face from an unfamiliar one;
- anticipate what happens when an object is dropped;
- learn that one action makes a toy respond;
- notice when a familiar pattern changes;
- reach differently after repeated failure;
- retain an expectation;
- generalize from one episode to another.

None of this establishes the full architecture proposed in this book.

It does not prove that every one of the six ways of changing, three ways of becoming enough, and three ways of keeping is already present before speech.

That remains an empirical question.

But it gives us a strong starting point:

> **Language does not enter an empty field.**

It enters a life in which differences, actions, failures, successes, relations, and expectations are already being organized.

The child is not waiting for language before anything can become enough.

## A toy before a word

Imagine a simple toy with two large buttons.

Pressing the blue button makes a light flash.

Pressing the yellow button does nothing.

At first the child presses both.

Then, gradually, behaviour changes.

The blue button is approached more often.

Something has been learned.

We can ask the same ordinary questions we used before.

What was being worked out?

Which action changes the toy?

What changed?

A useful difference became operative.

What made it enough?

Perhaps repeated success with one button became sufficient.

What remained?

An expectation, a preference, or a usable ability.

The child need not formulate any of this.

The architecture concerns what the event does, not whether the child has a theory of the event.

> **Instantiating a structure is not the same as being able to describe the structure.**

We use grammar long before we can explain grammar.

We balance our bodies long before we can explain balance.

The same may be true of the structures by which local matters become enough.

## Learning changes the next opening

Development is not simply the accumulation of more contents.

Each achievement changes the field from which later achievements begin.

A child who has learned that objects continue to exist when hidden approaches hiding differently.

A child who can crawl has access to different problems from one who cannot.

A child who can point can recruit another person into a task.

A child who can name a missing object can open a problem socially that previously had to be solved through searching alone.

A child who can read one word can enter a sentence that was previously unavailable.

Learning therefore does more than fill an internal store.

It changes what can become open next.

This is the developmental importance of traversal.

Earlier achievements restructure later beginnings.

The child's present is full of compressed history.

## First language does not merely label a finished world

If language enters an already moving architecture, first-language learning cannot simply be the attachment of words to concepts that are completely formed in advance.

Language changes the activity itself.

A child who can say:

> “Again”

can preserve a demand across time.

A child who can ask:

> “Why?”

can keep a relation open instead of accepting a result.

A child who learns:

> “Yesterday”

and:

> “tomorrow”

can organize events that are not present.

A child who learns:

> “because”

gains a public way to ask for and offer a path rather than only an answer.

A child who learns:

> “maybe”

can keep settlement from becoming stronger than the evidence supports.

Language therefore does not simply name distinctions the child already possesses in full.

It reorganizes which distinctions can be made, shared, revisited, combined, and carried forward.

First-language development is a process in which ways of making sense and ways of realizing them grow together.

## Language makes other people's paths available

Without language, a child can learn from demonstration, imitation, repetition, correction, and participation.

With language, another possibility expands dramatically.

Someone can preserve part of a path the child did not personally travel.

A parent can say:

> “Don't touch that pan. It's hot.”

The child need not discover the result through injury.

A teacher can explain a relationship that took earlier generations centuries to establish.

A story can preserve the consequences of choices the listener has never made.

A warning can alter action before failure occurs.

A proof can make a dependency reconstructible without the learner having to rediscover it unaided.

Language therefore changes development by changing what can be inherited.

A child can begin from achievements whose original paths happened elsewhere.

This does not eliminate the need to reconstruct.

It changes the starting point.

## Second language is a different problem

Now consider an adult learning another language.

They are not beginning in the same way.

They already know how to:

- request;
- refuse;
- explain;
- compare;
- warn;
- promise;
- tell stories;
- distinguish certainty from doubt;
- ask for reasons;
- give instructions;
- repair misunderstandings.

The learner may already possess many of the structures they want the new language to realize.

The problem is often:

> **How does this language let me do what I already know how to do?**

That is not the whole of second-language learning.

A new language can introduce unfamiliar distinctions, new cultural expectations, new conventional ways of organizing attention, and new patterns of interaction.

But the starting condition is different.

The learner is often remapping an already rich history into another realization system.

This helps explain why a learner can know exactly what they mean and still not know how to say it.

The underlying practical achievement is available.

The new realization is not.

## Why transfer can help and mislead

Existing knowledge can make second-language learning easier.

It can also create predictable errors.

If one language usually leaves a distinction to context while another requires it to be marked, a learner may repeatedly omit something the new language expects.

If one language uses word order to do work another language performs through endings, the learner may carry an old solution into a new system.

The mistake is not random.

The learner is trying to realize familiar achievements through an inherited set of resources.

Sometimes those resources transfer well.

Sometimes they do not.

This suggests a useful research question for MST:

> **Can some learning difficulties be predicted by the distance between two ways of realizing the same structural achievement?**

That is a hypothesis.

It should compete with ordinary linguistic explanations rather than replace them by declaration.

## Children make the hidden work visible

Adult competence is difficult to study because so much earlier work has become compressed.

We read words without consciously rebuilding letter-sound relations.

We catch implications without replaying the history that made them familiar.

We perform practiced actions while forgetting the route by which they became easy.

Children are useful because more of the construction remains visible.

A child learning to sort objects may visibly move from:

> “These look alike”

to:

> “This difference matters.”

A child learning a route may first follow landmarks one by one, then later seem simply to “know the way.”

A child learning arithmetic may count every item before later treating a result as something that stands.

Development lets us watch paths become compressed into starting points.

That is not a deficiency of childhood.

It is one of the clearest windows into how mature ease is built.

## Teaching changes what survives

Suppose two children both solve the same puzzle.

One is immediately told the next answer.

The other is asked:

> “How did you know?”

That question may change what survives.

The first child may keep the result.

The second may preserve more of the path.

Or the question may impose unnecessary verbal burden and interfere with a skill that is still becoming stable.

There is no universal rule that more explanation is always better.

The question is functional:

> **What will this learner need later?**

If the future demands routine performance, ability may matter most.

If it demands transfer, repair, or teaching, path preservation may become more important.

If it demands rapid recall, a stable result may be enough.

Development therefore connects naturally to the third local question:

> **What do we need to keep?**

That question now becomes the subject of the next chapter.

## Growing into a world already worked out

A child does not only accumulate personal achievements.

The child grows into an environment full of earlier ones.

Doors have handles.

Numbers have symbols.

Roads have signs.

Games have rules.

Tools embody solutions.

Adults carry expectations.

Books wait on shelves.

The world has already been organized by other people's completions.

Development therefore has two histories at once.

There is the child's own sequence of achievements.

And there is the inherited field into which those achievements enter.

The two become increasingly entangled.

Eventually it becomes hard to tell where personal discovery ends and inherited organization begins.

That is not a flaw.

It is what human development is like.

By adulthood, a person may carry thousands of answers, relations, and abilities whose original paths are partly or completely invisible.

Which brings us to a more precise question.

When something has become enough, what can still be there tomorrow?

## Continuity note

Keep memory mechanisms distinct from Persistence Mode. Introduce result/path more deeply, then show conversion: explanation into skill, skill into words, reason into rule, and expertise as compressed earlier work. Teaching should appear as selective decompression.

# Chapter 6 — What We Keep

You learn how to tie a knot.

A week later, three different things might be true.

You might remember:

> “The reef knot is the one I need.”

You might understand how the two loops cross and why reversing one changes the structure.

You might simply be able to tie it.

These are not the same achievement.

You can have one without fully having the others.

Someone can remember an answer and not understand the connection.

Someone can understand the connection and still lack the practiced ability.

Someone can perform skillfully and struggle to explain what they are doing.

This chapter is about that difference.

When something has become enough:

> **What remains?**

## Keep the answer

Suppose a mechanic tests three fuses and discovers which one has failed.

Tomorrow, the mechanic may remember:

> “Fuse 3 was the problem.”

The result stands.

It can be used as a starting point.

MST calls this **Standing**.

The formal word is useful, but the ordinary question is simpler:

> **Did we keep the answer?**

Standing includes more than verbal facts.

A decision can stand.

A classification can stand.

A measurement can stand.

A theorem can stand.

A diagnosis can stand.

What matters is that something is now available as settled enough to begin from.

## Keep the connection

Now suppose the mechanic remembers more.

Fuse 3 failed because water entered through a damaged seal, and the location of the seal explains why this fuse was affected while the others were not.

The relation remains.

That relation may matter when another failure appears.

MST calls this **Bearing**.

Again, the public question is easier:

> **Did we keep the connection?**

Bearing matters whenever the achieved result remains what it is because a relation, dependency, order, contrast, source, or proportion continues to organize it.

This can support transfer.

If one part of the relation changes, the person may be able to predict what follows.

## Keep the ability

Now suppose the mechanic can actually replace and reseal the component.

The achievement survives as something that can be done.

MST calls this **Efficacy**.

The ordinary question:

> **Did we keep the ability?**

Ability can survive without full verbal access.

A person may know how to ride a bicycle without being able to describe the balancing corrections involved.

A pianist may execute a sequence they would struggle to state note by note.

A craft worker may recognize the correct pressure through practice rather than explicit calculation.

What remains is real even when it is difficult to put into words.

## Persistence is not simply memory strength

Someone can remember a statement very strongly and still be unable to use it.

Someone can weakly recall the wording of a rule while remaining able to perform the skill.

Someone can forget the final answer but retain enough of the relation to derive it again.

These cases show why the three forms should not simply be ranked as:

> more memory / less memory.

They support different futures.

An answer supports one kind of continuation.

A relation supports another.

An ability supports another.

The empirical question is whether these differences can be reliably separated under controlled testing.

If they cannot, the distinction weakens.

But conceptually, the need for the distinction is clear.

“What remained?” cannot be answered well by one number called memory.

## Result and path return

The distinction between result and path cuts across what remains.

You may keep a result in Standing:

> “The answer is 18.”

You may keep a path that lets you reconstruct the answer:

> “There are three groups of six.”

You may keep an ability that embodies a path without making it verbally accessible.

Result and path are therefore not simply extra Persistence Modes.

They describe different depths of what has survived.

A result says:

> **Begin from here.**

A path says:

> **Here is enough of how we got here to work it out again.**

Both matter.

They matter differently.

## Compression

Once a path has been travelled often enough, much of it can disappear from conscious use.

A learner sounding out a word may begin:

> c — a — t.

Later the word is read immediately.

A beginner driving a car consciously checks mirror, signal, position, speed.

An experienced driver performs many of these adjustments with far less explicit attention.

A novice programmer may mentally rehearse each step of a pattern that an expert later recognizes almost at once.

This is **compression**.

Earlier traversal has become present structure.

Compression is one of the main ways learning becomes efficient.

Without it, expertise would be impossible.

But compression can make its own history difficult to recover.

## Expertise is not merely knowing more

An expert often starts farther ahead.

The difference is not always a larger pile of facts.

The expert sees a situation through distinctions built by earlier work.

A radiologist may notice a pattern that a novice does not yet know how to separate from background.

A mechanic may hear a diagnostic sound before a beginner knows which sounds matter.

A mathematician may recognize which theorem makes a problem tractable.

Years of prior traversal have changed the opening field.

This is why expertise can look mysterious.

The expert appears to leap.

But the leap often rests on compressed history.

> **Expertise is partly earlier work made available as a shorter starting point.**

## Expertise can lose its path

The same compression that produces speed can create teaching problems.

Ask an expert:

> “How did you know?”

The answer may be:

> “It just looked wrong.”

That may be completely sincere.

The ability remains.

The reconstructible path has weakened.

This produces the classic difficulty of expert instruction.

The expert can do what the learner cannot.

But the expert may no longer have easy access to the intermediate distinctions the learner needs.

Teaching then requires **decompression**.

The teacher must make some hidden path visible again.

Not necessarily the original historical path.

A teachable path.

Enough structure for another person to rebuild the achievement.

## An answer can become a skill

Persistence can change mode.

A written instruction begins as something that stands:

> “Place the blade at this angle.”

Through practice, the instruction may become a bodily ability.

Standing becomes Efficacy.

A relational explanation can become a rule:

> “When pressure rises here, flow changes there.”

Later this may be compressed into:

> “Check pressure first.”

Bearing has contributed to Standing.

A practiced skill can later be analyzed and verbalized.

Efficacy can produce new Standing and Bearing.

These conversions are not automatic.

But they matter because human learning is full of them.

What survives from one event can later be rebuilt in another form.

## Teaching is selective decompression

A good teacher does not reproduce everything they ever learned.

They choose which hidden structure the learner needs.

Suppose a child repeatedly gets a multiplication result wrong.

The teacher could provide the answer.

That preserves a result.

The teacher could draw equal groups.

That exposes a relation.

The teacher could practice until recall becomes fast.

That builds ability.

Which intervention is right depends on what should remain afterward.

This is why:

> **What do we need to keep?**

is a practical design question, not merely a theoretical one.

Teaching becomes clearer when the intended future is explicit.

## Forgetting can affect different things differently

People do not lose all parts of an achievement together.

The exact wording of an explanation may disappear while the relation survives.

The reason may disappear while the action remains fluent.

The action may become rusty while the rule is still remembered.

A result may be forgotten while enough path remains to reconstruct it.

This suggests that forgetting should sometimes be studied not only as loss of amount but as change in what kind of continuation remains possible.

What can still be done?

What can still be derived?

What can still be taught?

What can still be checked?

Those are Persistence questions.

## What remains can leave the person

A result can be written down.

A relation can be diagrammed.

A procedure can be recorded.

A skill can be partly externalized into a tool, jig, checklist, template, or machine.

Once this happens, persistence is no longer confined to biological memory.

Something achieved in one life can remain available outside that life.

A notebook remembers an answer differently from a person.

A proof preserves a path differently from a mathematician.

A tool can embody a solution without explaining it.

A checklist can carry Standing.

A diagram can preserve Bearing.

A machine setting can preserve part of Efficacy.

This enlarges the question again.

If what we keep can leave us, how can it survive time?

## Continuity note

Chapter 7 should begin with the striking fact that someone dead for centuries can still alter what a living person can work out. Distinguish artifact survival from understanding survival. Writing should be framed as temporal infrastructure for rebuilding: results, paths, proofs, archives, manuals, notation. Introduce dormant persistence only after the ordinary phenomenon is clear.

# Chapter 7 — How Understanding Outlives Us

Someone who died hundreds of years ago can still change what you are able to work out today.

That fact is so ordinary that it is easy to miss how strange it is.

A mathematician reads an old proof.

A musician reads a score.

A builder consults a drawing.

A cook follows a recipe.

A historian opens a letter.

A programmer reads source code written by someone they never met.

The original person is gone.

The original conversation is gone.

The original moment of insight is gone.

And yet something remains capable of reorganizing a living person's present.

How?

## A mark can survive without understanding surviving

Imagine a page in a language nobody can read.

The page survives perfectly.

The ink remains.

The symbols remain.

But if nobody can reconstruct what they do, the original achievement may be practically unreachable.

This is why physical survival is not enough.

Likewise, a manual can survive while nobody can perform the craft it describes.

A formula can survive while the assumptions that made it valid disappear.

A computer program can survive while the machine or environment required to run it no longer exists.

An archive can preserve millions of records while losing the practices needed to interpret them.

So the important question is not:

> Did the artifact survive?

It is:

> **Did enough survive for someone to work the relevant thing out again?**

## Writing separates achievement from presence

Speech usually disappears quickly.

Writing changes the temporal problem.

A person can work something out at one time.

They can leave marks.

Another person can encounter those marks much later.

The original speaker need not be present.

This creates a new pattern:

\[
\text{earlier achievement}
\rightarrow
\text{durable realization}
\rightarrow
\text{time passes}
\rightarrow
\text{new reader}
\rightarrow
\text{new reconstruction}.
\]

The marks do not contain the earlier understanding as a finished object.

They provide constraints from which another person may reconstruct enough of it.

Writing is powerful because it allows **delayed reconstruction**.

## A theorem and a proof preserve different things

A theorem statement can survive on one line.

Its proof may require pages.

If later readers need only to use the theorem, the short result may be enough.

If they need to verify it, teach it, generalize it, or repair an error, the proof matters.

This is the result/path distinction again.

A theorem statement is powerful compression.

A proof preserves more of the route.

Neither is universally better.

The right preservation depends on the future use.

This gives us a practical archival principle:

> **Preserve the path where future continuation may depend on being able to rebuild the result.**

## A recipe is not a meal

A recipe can survive a cook.

But it does not contain cooking.

It must be reconstructed through ingredients, tools, timing, senses, practice, and local conditions.

A reader may follow every written step and still fail because the recipe assumes knowledge that was never written down.

What does “fold gently” mean to someone who has never done it?

How hot is “medium heat” on a different stove?

What does “until done” look like?

Writing often preserves only part of the path.

Other parts remain in practice, convention, tool design, apprenticeship, shared background, or bodily skill.

This surrounding support is part of what makes reconstruction possible.

## What writing is good at

Writing is extraordinarily good at preserving Standing.

Names.

Dates.

Results.

Rules.

Decisions.

Definitions.

Measurements.

Statements.

Writing can also preserve Bearing.

A proof can show dependency.

A diagram can preserve relation.

A scientific paper can connect evidence to claim.

A legal judgment can record not only the outcome but the reasoning that made it stand.

Writing can sometimes support Efficacy.

A manual can help someone act.

A score can help someone perform.

A set of instructions can restore a process.

But written Efficacy usually depends on reconstruction by a capable person in a suitable environment.

The page does not act by itself.

## Dormant persistence

An unread book is not actively changing anybody.

Yet it can remain capable of doing so.

A proof sits on a shelf.

A map waits in an archive.

A manual remains in a drawer.

A software repository sits unused.

The achievement is neither gone nor currently active.

Something of it is dormant.

This is a useful way to think about writing.

Writing creates **dormant persistence**: externally stabilized structure that may later become active again if the right reader, tools, practices, context, and questions return.

Dormancy is not guaranteed survival of understanding.

It is preserved possibility.

## Reconstruction requires an ecology

Suppose an old engineering drawing survives.

To use it, a later person may need:

- the notation;
- the measurement system;
- the material conventions;
- the relevant tools;
- knowledge of what the labels meant;
- understanding of the design assumptions.

The drawing is only one component.

Reconstruction happens inside an ecology.

The same is true of software.

Source code may survive but depend on:

- libraries;
- operating systems;
- hardware;
- data formats;
- build tools;
- documentation;
- institutional knowledge.

Remove enough of that ecology and the artifact becomes difficult to reactivate.

This is why preservation must be about more than files.

It must ask what future reconstruction requires.

## Archives are fields of possible re-completion

An archive is often imagined as a place where the past is stored.

A better view is:

> **an archive is a field of possible reconstructions.**

Some items preserve results.

Some preserve paths.

Some preserve context.

Some preserve disagreement.

Some preserve evidence from which later people can reopen old conclusions.

The most valuable archive is not necessarily the one that stores the most material.

It is the one that preserves enough of the right structures for later continuation.

That can include the ability to challenge the inherited result, not merely reproduce it.

## Cultural transmission is not copying

A child reads an old story.

A scientist reproduces an experiment.

An apprentice learns an inherited technique.

A community repeats a ritual.

None of these is simple copying.

Each generation reconstructs.

Some parts change.

Some parts disappear.

Some are made explicit.

Some become compressed.

Some are reopened.

Some are converted from words to skills, from skills to tools, from tools back to explanation.

Culture persists through repeated reconstruction.

That makes transmission creative even when fidelity matters.

The past does not simply remain.

It is repeatedly made operative again.

## The dead can still create openings

This is the deeper significance of writing.

Someone works something out.

Something of that achievement is externalized.

The person disappears.

Later, another person encounters what remains.

A new distinction becomes visible.

A new question becomes possible.

A new ability develops.

A new disagreement begins.

The past changes the opening conditions of the present.

That is how one life can alter another without co-presence.

And once this process is organized systematically—through schools, archives, standards, laws, laboratories, professional roles, libraries, tools, and procedures—it becomes larger than writing.

It becomes institutional.

## Continuity note

Chapter 8 should begin from the fact that nobody starts from zero. Show civilization as inherited non-zero starting conditions. Institutions should distribute answers, relations, abilities, paths, checking, and authority across people and artifacts. Make “rich in results, poor in paths” central. Introduce the question “When are we entitled to begin from something we did not work out ourselves?” and let “when it still holds” become unavoidable.

# Chapter 8 — Nobody Starts From Zero

Nobody alive invented language from nothing.

Nobody alive reconstructed mathematics from the first act of counting.

No doctor rebuilt medicine from unaided observation.

No engineer personally derived every measurement standard, material property, manufacturing process, or software dependency they use.

No child invents roads, maps, clocks, alphabets, laws, books, or tools before beginning life among them.

Human beings begin inside work already done.

Civilization depends on that fact.

If every generation had to start over, almost nothing complex could survive.

The question is not whether we inherit.

We do.

The question is:

> **When are we entitled to begin from something we did not work out ourselves?**

## Non-zero starting points

A student uses a theorem proved long before they were born.

A surgeon relies on anatomical knowledge assembled across centuries.

A driver trusts that a bridge was built to standards they have never inspected.

A programmer imports a library they did not write.

A citizen follows a measurement, a law, or a procedure whose history they only partly know.

These are **non-zero starting points**.

They allow later activity to begin from achieved structure rather than raw uncertainty.

This is one of civilization's greatest powers.

The past changes the starting conditions of the present.

## Inheritance can also preserve error

But inheritance alone does not make a starting point good.

A bad rule can be inherited.

An obsolete procedure can be inherited.

A mistaken belief can be inherited.

A once-useful standard can persist after technology changes.

Authority can outlive the conditions that earned it.

So:

> **Inherited does not mean warranted.**

A civilization must do more than preserve.

It must preserve in ways that still support correction.

## Institutions distribute what no individual can carry

Consider a hospital.

No one person contains the whole institution.

Different forms of persistence are distributed.

A chart preserves results.

A specialist preserves relations.

A practiced team preserves abilities.

A protocol compresses earlier decisions.

A laboratory preserves measurement procedures.

An archive preserves paths and precedent.

Training converts written Standing into practiced Efficacy.

Audit and review provide ways to reopen what no longer works.

The institution continues because these elements support one another.

An institution is therefore not merely a group with memory.

It is an organized arrangement for preserving, distributing, reconstructing, and revising what earlier work made possible.

## Rich in results, poor in paths

An organization can accumulate many answers while losing how those answers were earned.

It may have:

- policies;
- checklists;
- decisions;
- standards;
- templates;
- approved values.

But little surviving explanation of:

- why they were adopted;
- what assumptions they depended on;
- what alternatives were rejected;
- what evidence supported them;
- what would make them obsolete.

Such an organization is **rich in results and poor in paths**.

This can work surprisingly well while circumstances remain stable.

Then conditions change.

Suddenly nobody knows which rules can safely be altered.

The organization becomes brittle.

Path poverty is often invisible during routine continuation.

It appears during disruption.

## Why authority exists

Compression creates enormous efficiencies.

We cannot verify everything personally.

At some point, we rely on other people, institutions, instruments, standards, or procedures.

That is not automatically irrational.

Authority becomes necessary whenever the full path is too expensive to replay.

The real issue is whether the system preserves enough warrant that trust is not arbitrary.

A theorem can be cited without re-proving it because mathematical practice preserves methods of checking.

A measurement can be used because calibration systems exist.

A certified component can be trusted because testing, traceability, standards, and accountability compress the manufacturing path.

The whole history need not be replayed.

But enough of the structure that earned the result must remain available somewhere.

## What makes a starting point lawful?

A lawful starting point is not one that can never be challenged.

It is one from which continuation is presently justified.

That justification may rest on:

- evidence;
- proof;
- reliable procedure;
- skill;
- verification;
- institutional checks;
- stable environmental conditions;
- inherited practice that continues to perform successfully.

The important word is **presently**.

A good starting point can stop being good.

A rule can be correct under one set of conditions and dangerous under another.

This brings back one of the book's simplest questions:

> **Can we still rely on this?**

Civilization depends not only on retaining answers.

It depends on knowing when their conditions have changed.

## When an old answer stops being enough

Imagine a building standard written decades ago.

It was careful.

It was tested.

It worked.

Then materials change.

Climate conditions change.

Building methods change.

The old standard may still contain valuable knowledge.

But its authority cannot come merely from age.

We must ask:

> What conditions made this standard good enough?

> Do those conditions still hold?

If yes, continuation may be reasonable.

If no, the matter must become open again.

Reopening does not mean the old standard was foolish.

It means its period of justified use has ended.

This is one of the most important patterns in the whole book.

## Civilization must avoid two opposite failures

A society can fail by starting over unnecessarily.

If every generation distrusts every inherited achievement, complexity collapses.

But a society can also fail by continuing too long.

If inherited results remain authoritative after the conditions that earned them disappear, history becomes rigidity.

So civilization must avoid:

> **starting over when it does not need to**

and:

> **continuing when it no longer should.**

Those are opposite errors.

A mature institution has to regulate between them.

## The path helps us know when to reopen

This is where path preservation becomes more important than nostalgia for process.

A path can tell us:

- what assumptions mattered;
- what evidence was decisive;
- what alternatives were considered;
- what dependencies supported the result.

That helps answer a new question:

> **What would have to change before this result should be checked again?**

A path is therefore useful not only because it tells us how we got here.

At its strongest, it helps tell us when “here” is no longer a good place to begin.

That will become central later.

## Inheritance grows beyond people

Civilization distributes starting points through:

- books;
- schools;
- laws;
- tools;
- roads;
- standards;
- databases;
- rituals;
- scientific methods;
- software;
- institutions.

Increasingly, it also distributes them through machines that generate new text, code, summaries, classifications, and recommendations.

That changes the scale of the problem.

A machine-generated answer may become a starting point for a human.

Or for another machine.

It may be inherited thousands or millions of times.

The old question therefore acquires new urgency:

> **What deserves to become the next starting point?**

That is where artificial intelligence enters.

# Chapter 9 — When an Answer Looks Finished but Isn't

A machine gives you an answer.

It is fluent.

It is well organized.

It sounds certain.

It even includes reasons.

What has actually happened?

Perhaps the answer is correct.

Perhaps the reasons are sound.

Perhaps the matter is genuinely settled enough for the purpose.

But none of those things follows from polish.

A finished-looking answer and a completed piece of sense-making are not the same thing.

Artificial intelligence makes this difference unusually visible because it can produce the outward form of completion at enormous speed.

That forces us to ask:

> **When does an answer deserve to become the next starting point?**

## Surface closure

Human beings are highly responsive to signs of finish.

A confident tone.

A clean list.

A final sentence.

A summary.

A recommendation.

A completed form.

These signals are useful because genuine work often has to mark that it is done.

But the signals can be produced without the underlying achievement.

That creates **surface closure**.

The matter looks settled.

Structurally, it may still be open.

AI makes surface closure cheap.

This is not because machines are uniquely deceptive.

Humans do it too.

AI simply scales the phenomenon.

## Four ways apparent completion can fail

### The answer is wrong

The simplest failure.

The response settles on an incorrect result.

The danger increases when fluency makes the result easy to inherit.

### The reason is invented

The answer may even be correct.

But the explanation does not preserve the real path.

This matters if somebody later relies on that explanation to transfer, verify, or adapt the result.

### The claimed ability is not there

A system can describe how to perform an action without being able to perform it.

It can say:

> “I checked.”

when no checking occurred.

Or produce plausible code that fails under execution.

Standing is presented as if Efficacy had been established.

### The question is closed too early

The system gives a definite answer when the evidence is incomplete, conditions are changing, or the matter should remain open.

This may be the most important failure.

The problem is not merely wrong content.

It is incorrect stopping.

## Counterfeit completion

These failures share a deeper pattern.

The outward signals of completion appear without the conditions that would make the completion earned.

We can call this **counterfeit completion**.

The term does not mean deliberate fraud.

It names a structural mismatch:

> **the realization says “done” before the matter has become enough.**

A counterfeit completion may look excellent.

That is precisely why it matters.

## Why hallucination is only one part of the problem

AI discussions often focus on hallucination: fabricated or incorrect claims.

That is important.

But the deeper problem is broader.

A system can be factually correct and still produce a poor starting point.

It may:

- omit the conditions under which the answer applies;
- suppress uncertainty;
- preserve the result while losing the path;
- give an answer where a question should remain open;
- summarize away the assumptions needed for future checking;
- state an action as completed when only a description was generated.

So the issue is not simply:

> Is the answer true?

It is also:

> **Has enough been done for this answer to guide what happens next?**

## AI produces inheritable starting points

This connects AI directly to civilization.

A human reads an AI summary and treats it as a basis for action.

A programmer accepts generated code.

A researcher uses an extracted claim.

A second AI system consumes an answer from the first.

The output becomes inherited structure.

At machine scale, small completion errors can propagate widely.

A brittle result can become the opening condition for thousands of later events.

That is why completion quality becomes historically important.

## A result without a path

AI systems are especially capable of producing compressed results.

That can be useful.

A user may not need the whole path.

But compression becomes dangerous when the missing path is required for:

- verification;
- adaptation;
- teaching;
- safety;
- reopening.

A summary may preserve:

> “Use treatment X.”

while removing:

> “Only under conditions A, B, and C.”

The result survives.

Its boundary does not.

That is exactly the kind of inheritance problem Chapter 8 prepared us to see.

## The system should sometimes refuse to settle

An intelligent system cannot be defined only by its ability to answer.

Sometimes the correct move is to preserve openness.

It may need to say, in ordinary language:

> “There isn't enough here yet.”

> “That depends on a condition we haven't checked.”

> “I can give a provisional result, but this should not be treated as settled.”

> “The old answer may no longer apply.”

This is not failure to complete.

It can be successful control of completion.

A system that always closes will eventually close what should have remained open.

## Checking what still holds

Suppose an AI system retrieves an old procedure.

The procedure worked when it was written.

Before recommending it, the system should ask:

> What did this depend on?

> Are those conditions still present?

The same applies to:

- old laws;
- old code;
- old scientific findings;
- old safety guidance;
- old institutional policies.

The intelligence lies partly in regulating inherited use.

An answer is not safe merely because it once deserved to stand.

## Completion control

This gives us a new engineering problem.

Instead of optimizing only for plausible output, a system can be designed around questions such as:

1. What is still open?
2. What needs to change?
3. What would make it enough?
4. What must remain afterward?
5. What should this make possible next?
6. When should it be checked again?

A system that can expose these questions is not automatically trustworthy.

But it makes the structure of stopping inspectable.

This can support human judgment.

## Human agency remains central

No architecture of completion can remove human responsibility for deciding purposes, values, acceptable risks, or what kind of consequence matters.

A medical decision and a crossword clue do not share the same cost of premature closure.

A safety system and a creative brainstorm should not use the same standard of enoughness.

Human beings still determine what matters.

What an explicit architecture can do is make assumptions easier to inspect.

It can ask:

> What are we treating as settled?

> Why?

> What survived?

> What would make us reopen it?

Those questions are useful whether the system is human, artificial, or mixed.

## From AI to deliberate design

AI makes the problem unavoidable because it turns completion-like output into a scalable technology.

But the problem is not fundamentally about AI.

Teachers design when explanations should be enough.

Engineers design when checks should be enough.

Scientists design when evidence should be enough.

Meetings design when discussion should become decision.

Translators design what must survive change of language.

Institutions design what later people may safely begin from.

We have been engineering enoughness all along.

The next question is whether we can do it more explicitly.

Show that engineering intelligibility means shaping the full loop, not merely selecting one of the 54. Include measurement, failure, reopening, and a small “completion record” style prototype. End by making the whole process visible enough for Chapter 11 to name what the book has actually been about.

# Chapter 10 — Designing for Enough

A teacher decides whether to give another example.

A mechanic decides whether the repair has been tested enough.

A scientist decides whether evidence supports a claim strongly enough to publish.

A meeting chair decides whether discussion is finished enough to make a decision.

A translator decides whether the target text preserves enough for the reader to continue correctly.

A programmer decides whether a test suite has covered enough.

These people are already designing conditions of enoughness.

They may not use that phrase.

They do not need to.

The structure is present in the work.

What changes if we make it explicit?

## Six questions

The practical architecture of this book can be carried by six questions.

> **1. What are we trying to work out?**

> **2. What needs to change?**

> **3. How will we know when it is enough?**

> **4. What do we need to keep?**

> **5. What will this let us do next?**

> **6. When should we check it again?**

These questions are deliberately ordinary.

They can be used by a child.

They can also support formal specification.

Their power lies in sequence.

Together, they prevent several common failures.

## What are we trying to work out?

Many bad processes begin by solving the wrong problem well.

A meeting talks for an hour without agreeing what is actually open.

A student answers the question they expected rather than the one asked.

An AI system produces an excellent explanation of the wrong issue.

The first engineering task is therefore event definition.

What is live?

At what scale?

For whom?

Relative to what purpose?

If this is vague, “enough” becomes impossible to specify.

## What needs to change?

Once the matter is clear, the process should identify the kind of change required.

Do we need to:

- tell two things apart?
- open a blocked possibility?
- connect elements?
- fix a fault?
- put parts together?
- settle a question?

These correspond to the six current Transformation values.

The practical wording should usually come first.

The formal category can follow when useful.

A good intervention changes what the actual opening requires.

## How will we know when it is enough?

This is the stopping problem.

Will one decisive thing settle the matter?

Must several conditions come together?

Does success require something that currently blocks progress to stop governing?

Those are the public forms of:

- Focal;
- Distributed;
- Release.

Making this explicit can prevent both premature stopping and endless continuation.

A meeting should not keep talking merely because more can be said.

A safety check should not stop merely because one reassuring sign appears.

The topology of enoughness matters.

## What do we need to keep?

Not every completion needs to leave the same thing behind.

Sometimes the future needs:

- a result;
- a relation;
- an ability;
- a recoverable path;
- a boundary condition;
- some combination.

A document meant only to record a decision may not need the full path.

A scientific claim may.

A training programme may need to produce Efficacy rather than verbal recall.

A safety procedure may need the result and the condition that makes it valid.

This question turns persistence into a design variable.

## What will this let us do next?

Completion is not useful merely because something stopped.

Its importance is what it enables.

A well-designed completion makes the intended continuation possible.

So before settling something, ask:

> What should become possible afterward?

If the answer is unclear, the completion criterion may be poorly chosen.

This is particularly useful in education.

The target should not be:

> “The learner understood.”

It should be something observable:

> “The learner can now solve a new case.”

> “The learner can explain the relation.”

> “The learner can perform the procedure.”

> “The learner knows when to check the rule again.”

## When should we check it again?

Every result has a future.

Some are stable for a very long time.

Some depend on conditions that change quickly.

A good design therefore records not only:

> What have we decided?

but also:

> **What would make this decision need to become a question again?**

This single question can make institutions less brittle.

It can also make personal reasoning more intelligent.

Reopening becomes part of the design rather than an embarrassment after failure.

## A completion record

The simplest practical tool suggested by the architecture is a one-page **completion record**.

It asks:

### What is still open?

What exactly remains unresolved, broken, uncertain, unavailable, or undecided?

### What must change?

What difference has to occur?

### What would make that change enough?

What observable condition would let us continue?

### What must remain afterward?

Do we need an answer, a relation, an ability, a path, or several of these?

### What result should be preserved?

What may later be taken as established?

### What path should remain recoverable?

What would someone need in order to check, teach, rebuild, or repair this later?

### When would this no longer hold?

What changed condition should trigger checking again?

### What should this make possible next?

What action, inference, decision, or new problem should now become available?

This is not a new bureaucracy.

It should be used only where making the structure explicit improves the work.

The tool itself has to earn its place.

## Teaching

A teacher can use the six questions to design a lesson.

What is the learner trying to work out?

What change should occur?

What would count as enough?

What needs to remain tomorrow?

What should the learner be able to do next?

What changed circumstance should reveal whether the learner really understood the relation rather than memorized the answer?

This produces testable design.

## Meetings

Meetings often fail because they mix openings.

One person thinks the task is to explore.

Another thinks it is to decide.

Another thinks the decision was already made and the task is implementation.

The first question can expose this.

The third question can define when discussion is enough.

The fourth determines what should be recorded.

The sixth records conditions for reopening.

A decision can then become a usable starting point instead of a vague memory of conversation.

## Documents

A document can be designed around future reconstruction.

Who will use it?

What will they need to do?

What path must remain visible?

What context will they lack?

What assumptions should be recorded because future conditions may change?

This shifts documentation from:

> “Did we write it down?”

to:

> **“Can the future reader reconstruct what they need?”**

## Translation

Translation can use the same structure.

What is the source doing?

What must change in the target reader?

What counts as enough?

What must survive—result, relation, action, path, condition?

What should the target reader be able to do next?

Which contextual change would make the translation misleading?

This makes structural preservation practical.

## AI

AI systems can expose the same six questions internally or to users.

Not every response needs a visible checklist.

But high-consequence uses may benefit from explicit structure.

What is the live matter?

What evidence would be enough?

Which conditions are still unverified?

What should remain after the response?

What downstream action is being enabled?

What would make the answer need rechecking?

This does not solve trust automatically.

It gives trust something inspectable to attach to.

## Measurement

A framework becomes scientific when its distinctions support predictions that can fail.

The 54 should therefore not be protected by retrospective classification.

Experiments should specify a target form before participants encounter the task.

They should manipulate one axis while holding others as constant as possible.

They should test whether different forms produce different downstream consequences.

The larger dynamics should also be tested.

Does preserving a path improve adaptation when conditions change?

Does preserving when a rule holds improve calibrated reopening?

Does removing an earlier result alter a supposedly dependent later event?

Does structural preservation predict downstream action beyond surface similarity?

If not, the theory should change.

## Engineering the loop, not the cell

The 54 describe the local shape of becoming enough.

Engineering concerns a larger loop:

\[
\text{open}
\rightarrow
\text{change}
\rightarrow
\text{enough}
\rightarrow
\text{what remains}
\rightarrow
\text{what becomes possible next}
\rightarrow
\text{check again when needed}.
\]

The cell matters.

But the cell is one moment inside the process.

A designed system must care about the whole cycle.

## What has become visible

At the beginning of this book, the central puzzle was local.

There seemed to be recurring ways in which something became enough.

Then each chapter pushed outward.

Enoughness left something behind.

What remained changed what could happen next.

Language helped one person rebuild another person's achievement.

Translation showed that structure could survive a change of realization.

Development showed that every present opening has a history.

Persistence showed that answers, relations, and abilities can survive differently.

Writing allowed achievements to outlive their originators.

Institutions organized inherited starting points.

AI exposed the danger of answers that look complete before enoughness has been earned.

Engineering made the full process inspectable.

We can now finally ask:

> **What have we actually been looking at all along?**

That question belongs to the final chapter.

## Continuity note

It should reveal that the 54 are the local morphology of enoughness, while the larger theory concerns how achieved organization becomes a condition of future sense-making. The mature new centre is conditional inheritance: what remains may guide continuation only while the conditions that made it good enough still hold.

# Chapter 11 — What We Were Looking At All Along

Something is open.

Something changes.

The change becomes enough.

Something from it remains.

What remains changes what can happen next.

We continue from it while the important conditions still hold.

When they no longer do, we look again.

That is the whole movement in its simplest form.

It has been present from the beginning.

But the early chapters could only see parts of it at once.

Now we can see the architecture as a whole.

## The 54 were never the whole object

The first discovery was local.

There appeared to be recurring ways in which an unresolved matter becomes enough.

Three questions exposed the structure:

> What changed?

> What made it enough?

> What remained?

The current answers produce:

\[
6\times3\times3=54.
\]

The 54 remain central.

But their proper place is now clear.

They describe the **local shape of becoming enough**.

They do not describe the whole event history.

They do not encode the complete path.

They do not tell us who inherits the result.

They do not tell us how the result is reconstructed.

They do not tell us when it should be reopened.

They are the morphology of one decisive moment.

That is not a reduction of their importance.

It is what allows them to remain precise.

## Enoughness matters because it creates a future

A matter becomes enough and then something can happen that could not happen before.

A result can be used.

A relation can guide inference.

An ability can support action.

A path can be compressed into a shorter starting point.

A new question can become available.

This is why completion is not merely stopping.

Its significance is consequential.

A genuine local completion changes the future.

Chapter 2 named the relation between an earlier achievement and a later opening **traversal**.

The key distinction was simple:

> sequence is not enough.

One event can follow another without mattering to it.

Traversal occurs when what remains from the earlier event changes what the later event can sensibly ask, assume, notice, do, or regard as enough.

The future inherits something.

## What remains is how achievement enters history

The third local question—what remained?—turned out to have a larger temporal role.

An answer can remain.

A relation can remain.

An ability can remain.

Formally, MST calls these:

- Standing;
- Bearing;
- Efficacy.

But the larger point is ordinary.

> **What remains from one achievement can become part of another beginning.**

That is how a local event becomes historical.

A result no longer belongs only to the moment in which it was achieved.

It can be reused.

## Result and path create different futures

A result lets us continue efficiently.

A path lets us reconstruct.

This distinction became increasingly important as the book moved outward.

A result can be compressed into:

> “Begin here.”

A path preserves enough of:

> “This is how here became a good place to begin.”

Civilization needs both.

Without results, every generation has to replay too much.

Without paths, inherited results become brittle.

The path becomes especially important when:

- conditions change;
- the result is challenged;
- someone must be taught;
- a failure must be repaired;
- the original achievement must be reopened.

The route is not valuable merely because history is interesting.

It preserves reconstructibility.

## Language makes achievements reconstructible elsewhere

Words do not contain understanding.

They help another person build it.

That was Chapter 3's central claim.

A speaker cannot transfer a completed internal state directly.

They can alter another person's field.

A sentence can direct attention.

A question can reopen a matter.

An explanation can preserve a relation.

An instruction can support ability.

A proof can expose a path.

Language therefore mediates traversal.

It allows one person's achieved organization to become part of another person's beginning.

## Translation reveals structure beneath realization

Translation then forced a harder question.

If almost every word can change, what stays the same?

The answer cannot be:

> the exact linguistic form.

Sometimes the form must change dramatically for the same practical or structural achievement to survive.

Translation therefore exposed several different kinds of preservation:

- result;
- relation;
- path;
- local coordinate;
- action;
- future continuation;
- the conditions under which the result should still be relied upon.

This showed that realization and structure must remain distinct.

The carrier matters.

But it is not identical with what the carrier makes reconstructible.

## Development shows that every present opening has a past

A child's life made the temporal structure impossible to ignore.

A later problem is never approached from nowhere.

Earlier distinctions change what can be noticed.

Earlier actions change what can be attempted.

Earlier language changes what can be asked.

Learning is therefore not merely accumulation.

It is historical restructuring of future openings.

The mature person begins almost everywhere from non-zero conditions built by previous traversals.

Development is inheritance at the scale of a life.

## Expertise is compressed history

Chapter 6 showed how this history can become hard to see.

Experts often appear to skip steps.

The steps have not necessarily disappeared from the history of the competence.

They have become compressed.

Earlier work becomes present structure.

This is why expertise can be both fast and difficult to explain.

It is also why teaching can require decompression.

The learner needs enough of the hidden path to build what the expert now uses automatically.

## Writing lets achievement survive its achiever

Writing extended the time scale.

A person dies.

A mark remains.

A later person reconstructs something from it.

The achievement crosses separation in time.

But artifact survival is not enough.

A text can remain while its understanding disappears.

A manual can survive while nobody can perform the craft.

A program can survive while its environment disappears.

So what must persist is not simply information.

Enough of the relevant organization and its reconstruction conditions must survive.

Writing creates dormant possibilities of re-completion.

## Institutions distribute persistence

Civilization made the architecture collective.

A society cannot place every result, relation, ability, and path inside every person.

It distributes them.

Documents carry some things.

Experts carry others.

Tools embody others.

Institutions preserve procedures, roles, standards, checking mechanisms, training, authority, and routes of reopening.

Civilization allows later people to begin from achievements they did not personally earn.

This is one of its deepest powers.

It is also one of its deepest risks.

## AI reveals the danger of inherited appearance

Artificial intelligence made the distinction between completion and apparent completion impossible to ignore.

A machine can generate something that looks like a finished answer without establishing the structure that would make the answer a good starting point.

That is counterfeit completion.

The danger is historical.

The output may be inherited.

People or other machines may continue from it.

The central question becomes:

> **Does this deserve to become the next starting point?**

AI therefore exposes a problem already present everywhere else.

How do we regulate continuation from inherited results?

## Engineering makes the loop explicit

Once the process is visible, it can be designed.

The six public questions summarize the practical architecture:

1. What are we trying to work out?
2. What needs to change?
3. How will we know when it is enough?
4. What do we need to keep?
5. What will this let us do next?
6. When should we check it again?

These questions are not a simplified version of a hidden technical theory.

They are the theory at a usable resolution.

The formal architecture sits underneath them.

The 54 specify local morphology.

Traversal specifies dependence between events.

Result and path specify different survivor depths.

Reconstruction specifies how inherited organization becomes active again.

Reopening specifies how something that was once enough becomes open again.

And one final relation now becomes unavoidable.

## When does an inherited result still deserve to guide us?

Every chapter gradually depended on this question.

A route works until a bridge closes.

A medical procedure works until evidence or circumstances change.

A software system works until its environment changes.

A standard works while the conditions for which it was designed still obtain.

A legal precedent guides while the relevant applicability conditions still fit.

An AI summary may be useful until a key assumption disappears.

So what remains after completion cannot be described fully by asking only:

> What survived?

We must also ask:

> **When can we still rely on it?**

Every inherited result therefore has conditions under which it remains a good starting point.

Formally, we can describe those as its **validity conditions**.

But the ordinary language is better:

> **When does it still hold?**

If the relevant conditions continue to hold, we may continue.

If they do not, the matter may need to become open again.

This is the missing connection between inheritance and reopening.

## Reopening is not failure

Something can have been genuinely enough yesterday and need to be reopened today.

That does not make yesterday's completion unreal.

It means its conditions changed.

This matters because a theory of intelligent continuation must avoid two opposite errors.

### Reopen everything

Then no achievement remains usable.

We repeatedly start over.

Complexity becomes impossible.

### Reopen nothing

Then inherited achievements become rigid.

We continue from results after their conditions have expired.

Intelligence requires regulation between these extremes.

Continue while the relevant conditions still hold.

Reopen when they do not.

## The path can preserve the boundary

This also deepens the result/path distinction.

A path does not only tell us:

> How did we get here?

At its strongest, it can also tell us:

> **What did getting here depend on?**

And therefore:

> **What would have to change before we should stop beginning from here?**

This is one reason path-poor systems become brittle.

They may preserve the result but lose its boundary.

The answer survives longer than the conditions that made the answer lawful.

## Conditional inheritance

The project now has a name for the larger unity.

Inheritance is never simply:

> keep this forever.

It is:

> **continue from this while the conditions that earned it still hold.**

That is **conditional inheritance**.

The phrase need not become public vocabulary.

The ordinary language is enough:

> This worked before.

> Does it still hold now?

But theoretically, the point is deep.

History is not merely accumulated results.

It is accumulated conditional permission to begin from earlier achievements.

Civilization is not merely preserving more.

It is preserving enough to avoid two catastrophes:

> starting over unnecessarily

and:

> continuing from what no longer deserves to stand.

## Intelligibility has an architecture of inheritance

We can now say what the book has been approaching.

Making Sense Together began with a morphology of local enoughness.

What it discovered is larger.

A sense-making system encounters something still open.

Something changes.

The change becomes enough in a particular way.

Something of that achievement remains.

What remains can be compressed, converted, externalized, translated, reconstructed, taught, embodied, institutionalized, or inherited.

It changes what another event can begin from.

That inheritance remains good only while the relevant conditions still hold.

When they no longer do, intelligence requires reopening.

The deepest discovery is therefore not simply that there are fifty-four forms.

It is:

> **Intelligibility has an architecture of inheritance.**

We do not merely work things out.

We live from things already worked out.

What was worked out before changes what is possible now.

And intelligent continuation depends on knowing both when we may rely on what came before and when we must make it a question again.

## The whole architecture in ordinary words

Something is still open.

We work on it.

Something changes.

The change becomes enough.

We keep what the future needs.

What we keep changes what we can do next.

We do not have to start over.

But the answer only deserves to keep guiding us while the important things it depends on still hold.

When they change, we check again.

That is the movement.

## The same architecture formally

The local coordinate remains:

\[
c=(T,C,P)
\]

with the current morphology:

\[
6\times3\times3=54.
\]

A completed local event can be minimally represented as:

\[
E=(O,c,\Sigma)
\]

where:

- \(O\) is the opening;
- \(c\) is the local completion coordinate;
- \(\Sigma\) is what remains consequentially available.

A survivor may preserve result structure and path structure.

A traversal:

\[
E_i\leadsto E_j
\]

occurs when what survives from \(E_i\) materially changes the opening, sufficiency, warrant, or possible action of \(E_j\).

For an inherited survivor \(\Sigma_i\), let \(V_i\) denote the conditions under which it remains a lawful starting point for the continuation at issue.

Then, schematically:

\[
K_t\in V_i
\Rightarrow
\text{continuation may proceed}
\]

while:

\[
K_t\notin V_i
\Rightarrow
\text{reopening is required}.
\]

This formalism is not a second theory.

It is a sharper rendering of the same ordinary questions.
