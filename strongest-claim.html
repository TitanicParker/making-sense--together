Making Sense Together

The Strongest Claim

What We Can Certainly Say

The door closed. The door is closed. There is very little between those sentences, and enough between them to live by. One gives us something happening: a door passes from one condition into another. The other gives us a condition that now stands. Add a third sentence—the door can now close—and the door may still be wide open; what has changed is possibility. Say the door no longer closes and its present position hardly matters; something that once governed what could happen has gone. Say the door closes only when this catches and the closing belongs to a relation. We understand these differences before anyone explains them to us. That is partly why they are easy to miss.

Most of the time we look through understanding toward whatever has become understandable. We notice the door, the answer, the joke, the warning, the mistake, the promise. When understanding works, it gets out of its own way. Something becomes clear enough and we continue. But the continuation leaves an object behind that we can turn around and look at: the event by which the situation became intelligible enough for something else to follow.

What changed? What made the change enough? What remained?

Those questions do not have to be taught before they apply. Suppose somebody says something and you understand them. Before the utterance was taken up, its particular contribution was not yet available to you in the way it became available afterward. The difference may be enormous or almost nothing. It may take an hour or less than a second. You may acquire a fact, recognize a refusal, catch a joke, find the place being pointed at, understand an instruction, or finally grasp exactly what another person is wrong about. If nothing at all is different between the not-yet and the now, there is nothing for us to call an achievement of intelligibility. So we can ask what changed.

If we say that you understood rather than that you were still trying to understand, then something about the uptake was enough. Not enough for every purpose. Not final, certain, complete, true, or agreed by everyone. Enough for this. A fact can settle while the larger question remains open. Two people can understand one another well enough for their disagreement to become real. A mechanic can know where to look without yet knowing what will be found. Completion is local before it is grand. So we can ask what made the change enough.

And once the achievement has occurred, something is available because it occurred, even if only briefly. A determination can stand. A relation can now matter. A capacity can have changed. A question can be inherited. An old explanation can lose its grip. A person can know what to do next without retaining the sentence by which they learned it. What remains need not be a memory of words, and it need not remain forever. It is whatever form of availability the completed event leaves for what can follow. So we can ask what remained.

This is the strongest claim because it does not depend on any present taxonomy being perfect. The three questions are older than our answers to them. If a genuine local achievement of intelligibility has occurred, there is a difference between its opening and achieved situations, some respect in which that difference has become sufficient at the scale in question, and some way in which the achieved result is available as achieved. The questions do not create those facts. They let us inspect them.

That means the phenomenon is older than language. Before language, the world could already resist, yield, open, close, hold, fail and change what was possible. One animal could reach something another could not. A surface that could be crossed one way could not be crossed another. A previously successful move could stop working when the situation changed. Life does not wait for description. Situated creatures encounter consequential differences, alter their engagement, sometimes arrive at enough, and carry consequences of those arrivals forward.

Language enters later and begins to do something remarkable with those differences: it makes them recoverable between us. Imagine people brought together without a shared language. Hunger, danger, shelter, absence, failure, success, dependence, possibility and consequence have not disappeared while they wait for grammar. At first the situation can carry much of the burden. A gesture, a look, a hand on a latch, a repeated movement, a sound attached to a thing: while everyone is standing in front of the same door, that may be enough. But human life does not remain in front of the door. We speak about yesterday, warn somebody who was not there, tell a child what another person discovered, say what will happen if something else happens first, preserve instructions, revise agreements, inherit questions. The immediate scene can no longer do all the work. Language has to carry more of the difference.

A developing language need not reproduce one universal grammar, and it does not have to mark every distinction every time. The deeper pressure is simpler. Situated life keeps producing differences that matter, and communication keeps having to make enough of them recoverable. Once a language has stabilized a distinction, the next person can receive it without rediscovering it from nothing. Culture can turn the end of one person's difficult achievement into part of another person's beginning. Language is therefore not merely downstream. Once established, it becomes part of the world that later minds encounter. It can direct attention, preserve an achievement, sharpen a contrast and keep a question alive after the person who first asked it is gone.

Now the structure folds back on itself. We encounter something, make enough sense of it to continue, communicate what has become available, stabilize some of that communication, and hand it forward. What one generation had to notice can become ordinary equipment for the next. Something that once demanded concentrated thought can later become one piece of a larger thought. The products of making sense accumulate around us as words, tools, habits, measurements, recipes, warnings, maps, proofs, institutions, jokes and methods. The achievement disappears into what it makes possible.

This is also why every sentence that has ever successfully made sense to somebody else is an instance of the phenomenon. Not an instance of every form we may eventually distinguish, and not proof of a finished theory. An instance of the thing itself. Someone said something. Someone else took it up. Something became available that had not been available in that way before. At some scale the uptake was enough for the communicative act to function. Something could then follow from it. The content might be fire, no, I love you, two plus two is four, that is not what I meant, or there. The event underneath is different every time and structurally available every time. We can ask the three questions of the achievement.

We did not need to know this in order to do it. That is not a weakness in the claim. It is what gives the claim its depth. Blood circulated before anyone studied circulation. People spoke grammatically before grammar became an object of inquiry. A child can learn what to do without possessing a theory of learning. A crow can solve a practical relation without naming relation. Two people can understand a pointing gesture without either of them representing the conditions of successful uptake. A structure can be operative for a very long time before somebody stops using it long enough to look directly at what it is doing.

I came to that stopping place indirectly. I did not begin with these three questions. I kept finding structures that seemed more systematic than the explanations I had for them, and I kept being drawn back by their recurrence and their smallness. At one point a theorem became important to me for an almost embarrassingly simple reason: a theorem is a sentence. It says a very constrained kind of thing, under severe standards, but it does not cease to be a sentence. Once that boundary softened, I began trying to hold meaning apart from its ordinary journey long enough to see the journey itself. If meaning was a train, I wanted the train beside the tracks.

For a while I built a great deal of machinery around that suspicion. I wanted a sentence to yield a receipt. I wanted its movement exposed, its landing named, its structure numbered. Some of that machinery was useful and some of it was wrong. What mattered was that the suspicion survived changes to the instrument. Again and again, underneath the machinery, the same difficulty remained: we are very good at looking at what has become intelligible and much less accustomed to looking at the achieved intelligibility itself. The apparatus could be discarded. The object did not disappear.

The present work proposes recurring answers to the three questions: kinds of change, shapes by which a change becomes sufficient, and modes in which its result remains available. Crossing those answers produces fifty-four forms. That is a serious proposal, but it is not the strongest claim. The field can be tested. A distinction may need division. Another may be wrongly drawn. Another constitutive dimension may appear. If the map changes, the territory that made mapping possible is still there. The questions are deeper than the present answers.

What we can certainly say is therefore both smaller and larger than a classification system. Making sense is something that happens. Its happening has structure. We can place that structure in view. We can inspect a completed local achievement rather than only the answer, belief, sentence, solution or decision it leaves us with. We can ask what changed, what made that change enough, and what remained. We can compare the answers across a drawer repair, a mathematical proof, a disagreement, a bodily skill, a warning, a translation, a failed explanation, a promise, a bird with a tool, a child at a slope.

The fact that the questions apply does not mean we will always know the answers. Sometimes the event is gone. Sometimes the evidence is poor. Sometimes we choose the wrong scale. Sometimes we mistake what actually occurred. Those are difficulties of looking. They do not make the object vanish. The world is allowed to correct the description. The map is allowed to change. In fact, that is what keeps the inquiry alive.

The phenomenon has been happening for as long as something has become intelligible enough for something else to follow. We have lived among its products, spoken through them, inherited them, forgotten their origins and used them to make further sense. We did not need to know the structure in order to inhabit it. We only needed to notice it in order to begin studying it. There.
